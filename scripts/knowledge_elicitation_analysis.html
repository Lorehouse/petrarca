Section I

The Headline Finding

~3%
of available LLM context window used by review prompts
The rest is empty space where user knowledge could go

Voice elicitation generates the richest, most personal knowledge data in the entire system — the user’s actual words, their connections, their uncertainties, what stuck and what didn’t. Almost none of this data flows back into the system’s prompts or decisions.

The core disconnect

When generating a review card about “Aristotle and the Lyceum,” the LLM has zero knowledge that you found the Alexander-Aristotle relationship fascinating, connected it to Macedonian politics, wondered about Stagira being destroyed, or confused Nicomachus’s role. It receives only: knowledge_state: engaged, confidence: 0.45, last_score: partly.

The transcript is stored and forgotten. The system treats voice elicitation as a one-shot grading event rather than a continuous knowledge accumulation mechanism.

Each voice elicitation produces a rich LLM analysis. Here is where each field goes:

The conversion loss

Hours of rich voice data — the user’s actual words, their mental model, their connections and uncertainties — are reduced to two numbers: a score (knew/partly/missed) and a coverage percentage. That is the entire user model per topic.

What review prompts actually receive about the user

node_title + description → From curriculum (static)
source_text[:400] → First 400 chars of book source
3 known nodes in domain → Other topics user knows
temporal cross-refs → “Meanwhile in...” events
6 mastered key_facts → Facts user got right before
What’s missing
what user actually said connections they made their misconceptions
what they found interesting their open questions confidence per sub-topic
Section III

Two Modes of Elicitation

Voice elicitation serves fundamentally different purposes depending on temporal distance from learning:

Distant recall
Salience mapping

Byzantium, 1814, Karl XII, Henry VIII — topics studied long ago through many sources

  • Reveals what matters to the learner
  • Shows which connections they built naturally
  • Exposes their organizing framework
  • “Interesting” field is gold — original thinking
  • Less about gaps, more about personal salience
Fresh recall
Retention prevention

Chapter just read, podcast just heard — recent encoding, fact-rich

  • Without active processing, only gists survive
  • “Sicily was part of Ancient Greece” stays; names and sequences become mush
  • Specific dates, figures, sequences need reinforcement
  • “Missed” field directly identifies retention targets
  • The system’s core anti-forgetting function
“I don’t think I’ll ever forget that Sicily was dominated by Arabs around the year 1000, because those are gists linked to strong anchors. But all of the different names, sequences, when was Hiero the First, when was Archimedes — it’s all going to be a mush.”
Design implication

The system already distinguishes chapter_recall vs. curriculum recall at the UI level. This distinction should propagate to storage and downstream use: distant recall feeds salience scoring and personalization; fresh recall feeds retention targeting.

Section IV

The “Digital Twin” Gap

What a digital twin would know

  • “Stian finds the Alexander-Aristotle relationship fascinating”
  • “He consistently connects Macedonian politics to Athenian intellectual life”
  • “He confuses the chronology of Hiero I and Archimedes”
  • “His mental model of Byzantine history is organized around Constantinople as a city, not around emperors”
  • “When thinking about Aristotle, he naturally reaches for French neoclassical drama connections”
  • “He has strong knowledge of Arab Sicily but weak on Norman succession”

What the system currently knows

knowledge_state: "engaged"
confidence: 0.45
last_score: "partly"
review_count: 3

That’s the entire user model for a topic. The rich signal from voice elicitation is reduced to these fields.

Section V

What the Research Says

Recent research from research/knowledge-systems-deep-dive.md, research/knowledge-assessment-research.md, and research/academic-landscape-and-collaborations.md directly supports a richer approach:

Free Recall Reveals Knowledge Structure Tulving, Karpicke, Zemla & Austerweil
“Tell me what you know about Sicily” exposes how knowledge is organized — what clusters together, what sequences connect, what’s central vs. peripheral. Expert-novice differences aren’t amount but organization.
Actionable → Analyze transcript structure (clustering, ordering) not just content. Track organization quality over time as a learning metric.
Pathfinder Networks Goldsmith, Johnson & Acton (1991)
Similarity between learner’s knowledge network and expert curriculum graph (C metric) correlates with exam performance at r = .74 — the single best predictor of learning outcomes.
Actionable → Compare the connections made in voice transcripts against curriculum graph structure. The distance IS the learning metric.
SNAFU Zemla & Austerweil — Syracuse / Memory & Cognition 2026
Estimates semantic network structure from free recall using U-INVITE (random walk on semantic net). Can analyze individual-level knowledge structure from verbal fluency data.
Actionable → Apply SNAFU to voice transcripts to build per-domain knowledge graphs. Compare these against curriculum graphs monthly.
GENCAT: Generative Adaptive Testing Feng & Lan — UMass Amherst (Feb 2026)
Uses LLMs to both generate and predict student open-ended responses. A student’s latent knowledge vector conditions expected responses. 4.3% AUC improvement over traditional IRT.
Actionable → Closest academic system to Petrarca’s voice assessment. The idea of predicting expected responses given knowledge state, then comparing to actual transcript, is directly applicable.
Matuschak’s Pivot Andy Matuschak — Orbit / Salience prompts (2024–2026)
Key distinction: retrieval prompts test recall; salience prompts keep ideas top-of-mind so you notice opportunities to apply them. Standard SRS scheduling is wrong for salience. Orbit development has stalled.
Actionable → Curriculum framework nodes (“causes of Roman decline”) should activate when reading new material, not just be recallable. Different scheduling for salience vs. retrieval items.
Prior Knowledge Dominates Recht & Leslie (1988) — Journal of Educational Psychology
High-knowledge poor readers recalled more than low-knowledge good readers. Knowledge structure trumps reading skill for comprehension and retention.
Actionable → The factual scaffold built through voice elicitation IS the system’s primary value. Investing in richer knowledge storage directly improves everything downstream.
Historical Thinking Frameworks Seixas, Wineburg, AP History
Historical understanding isn’t just knowing facts — it’s sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, cause/consequence, perspective-taking. Voice transcripts reveal which of these thinking skills the learner employs naturally.
Actionable → When the user says “I wonder if Athens willingly joined Alexander’s campaign” that’s perspective-taking. Tag these thinking skills in the learner profile.
Section VI

Five Proposals

D
Inject existing voice data into review prompts
High impact Very low effort

The simplest possible improvement. When generating a review question for node X, query voice_transcripts for the most recent elicitation and append the existing captured, missed, interesting, and wonderings fields to the prompt.

Implementation: In generate_question() (review_engine.py:1315), add a query: SELECT llm_result FROM voice_transcripts WHERE node_id = ? AND source = 'elicitation' ORDER BY created_at DESC LIMIT 1. Parse the JSON, format as “LEARNER CONTEXT” section, append to prompt. ~300-500 extra tokens.

What changes: The review question can now target known gaps, build on connections the learner already made, and avoid re-testing what they clearly know. Zero new infrastructure — just a DB query and string formatting.

A
Learner Knowledge Profile per node
Very high impact Medium effort

After each voice elicitation, compile a structured “learner knowledge profile” that accumulates across elicitations. Not a replacement — a merge. Knowledge grows.

Schema:
node_id, domain_id
facts_known: [“Aristotle was student of Plato”, “Tutored Alexander”, ...]
facts_missed: [“Invented formal logic”, ...]
misconceptions: [“Father was tutor to Philip (actually physician to Amyntas III)”]
connections_made: [“Alexander-Aristotle personal relationship”, ...]
interests: [“French neoclassical drama reception”]
organizing_framework: “Biographical arc + political context”
thinking_skills: [“perspective-taking”, “contextualization”]

Research connection: This is the Petrarca equivalent of Pathfinder’s learner network. Each profile entry represents a node in the learner’s actual semantic network, with its connections and weights.

Cost: One Gemini Flash call per elicitation to merge new data into existing profile. ~200-500 tokens stored per node.

B
Cross-topic knowledge retrieval (RAG)
Very high impact Medium effort

When generating content about a topic, retrieve relevant snippets from all the user’s voice data — not just the current node.

Example: When generating a review card about “The Lyceum,” pull in what the user said about Plato (Aristotle’s teacher), Alexander (his student), Athens (its location), Arab transmission of Greek philosophy (its legacy).

Implementation: Use limbic.amygdala embeddings on transcript chunks (paragraph-level). Store in transcript_embeddings table. At review time, embed the node description, cosine-search top-k passages, include as “what the learner has said about related topics.”

Research connection: This is how SNAFU’s semantic network estimation works — by analyzing how concepts cluster across free recall episodes. The embedding similarity IS the semantic distance.

C
Entity-level knowledge aggregation
High impact Medium effort

For each entity (person, place, event) mentioned across all voice data, build an aggregated “what the user knows about X.”

Example for “Alexander the Great”:

  • From Aristotle elicitation: “Tutored by Aristotle, sent specimens back, destroyed Stagira”
  • From Sicily elicitation: “Part of the Hellenistic world after Alexander”
  • From Persian Empire (future): “Defeated Darius, spread Greek culture east”
Research connection: The entity-profiles-design.md already specifies a 4-layer scaffold: identity → context → connections → significance. Voice data populates all four layers with the user’s actual knowledge, not just curriculum definitions. The overlapping curricula vision means entity knowledge should be tracked per lens (Alexander-the-general vs. Alexander-the-patron-of-learning).
E
Domain knowledge summaries (“the digital twin”)
Very high impact Low effort (once A+B exist)

Periodically compile ALL voice data for a domain into a 1–2 page “learner knowledge portrait.” This becomes the primary context for all content generation in that domain.

Instead of passing raw node descriptions, pass: “Here’s what this specific learner knows, thinks, and cares about in Ancient Greece.”

Implementation: One Opus call per domain, run after every 3 new elicitations. Store as document. Include: knowledge map (what’s strong, what’s weak), organizing frameworks, cross-domain connections the learner makes, open questions, interests, misconceptions to address.

This is the actual digital twin — a document that represents you. It can be inspected, edited, and shared. It grows richer with every voice interaction.

The path

  1. Proposal D — immediate win. Query existing voice_transcripts.llm_result and inject into prompts. Can be done in half a session. Proves the concept.
  2. Proposal A — the foundation. Structured profiles that accumulate. This is what all other proposals build on.
  3. Proposal B — the connections. limbic embeddings on transcripts. Enables “what has the user said about topics related to X?”
  4. C + E emerge naturally from the infrastructure built in A + B.

Elicitation prompt enhancements (all proposals)

The VOICE_ELICITATION_PROMPT should also be enriched to extract more structured data:

  • Confidence tagging: “I’m pretty sure” vs. “I wonder if” — tag each fact with user’s apparent certainty
  • Framework detection: “What organizing principle does the learner use?” (chronological, biographical, geographic)
  • Connection classification: For each “interesting” item: temporal, causal, cross-domain, personal interpretation
  • Thinking skills: Sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, perspective-taking (per Seixas/Wineburg)
Section VIII

Open Questions

When should profiles be regenerated?

After every elicitation (incremental merge), or batched? Incremental is more responsive but risks drift from accumulation errors. A periodic full recompile (every 3 elicitations) might be more reliable.

Pre-existing vs. app-learned knowledge

Should the system distinguish “I knew this before Petrarca” from “I learned this through the app”? Distant recall of pre-existing knowledge is different from demonstrating retention of app-presented material. Both are valuable but for different purposes.

Filter bubble risk

If the system knows the user finds X interesting, should it surface more X-related content? This creates a filter bubble. Counter: the curriculum structure already provides breadth; personalization only affects how content is presented, not what is shown.

Voice notes integration

Open-ended voice notes (not tied to curriculum nodes) contain knowledge too. Should they feed the same profile system? They’re harder to route to specific nodes but may contain the most interesting cross-domain connections.

Measurement

How do we know if richer prompts produce better review cards? The publishable-experiments-research.md outlines N-of-1 methodology: A/B test enriched vs. standard prompts, measure retention at 2-week and 4-week intervals using Tau-U effect sizes. The voice elicitation itself is the measurement instrument — does knowledge structure improve faster with personalized cards?
