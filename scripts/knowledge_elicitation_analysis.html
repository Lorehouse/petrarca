Section I

The Headline Finding

~3%
of available LLM context window used by review prompts
The rest is empty space where user knowledge could go

Voice elicitation generates the richest, most personal knowledge data in the entire system — the user’s actual words, their connections, their uncertainties, what stuck and what didn’t. Almost none of this data flows back into the system’s prompts or decisions.

The core disconnect

When generating a review card about “Aristotle and the Lyceum,” the LLM has zero knowledge that you found the Alexander-Aristotle relationship fascinating, connected it to Macedonian politics, wondered about Stagira being destroyed, or confused Nicomachus’s role. It receives only: knowledge_state: engaged, confidence: 0.45, last_score: partly.

The transcript is stored and forgotten. The system treats voice elicitation as a one-shot grading event rather than a continuous knowledge accumulation mechanism.

The conversion loss

Hours of rich voice data — the user’s actual words, their mental model, their connections and uncertainties — are reduced to two numbers: a score (knew/partly/missed) and a coverage percentage. That is the entire user model per topic.

source_text[:400] → First 400 chars of book source
3 known nodes in domain → Other topics user knows
temporal cross-refs → “Meanwhile in...” events

what user actually said connections they made their misconceptions

Section III

Two Modes of Elicitation

Voice elicitation serves fundamentally different purposes depending on temporal distance from learning:

Distant recall
Salience mapping

Byzantium, 1814, Karl XII, Henry VIII — topics studied long ago through many sources

  • Reveals what matters to the learner
  • Shows which connections they built naturally
  • Exposes their organizing framework
  • “Interesting” field is gold — original thinking
  • Less about gaps, more about personal salience
Fresh recall
Retention prevention

Chapter just read, podcast just heard — recent encoding, fact-rich

  • Without active processing, only gists survive
  • “Sicily was part of Ancient Greece” stays; names and sequences become mush
  • Specific dates, figures, sequences need reinforcement
  • “Missed” field directly identifies retention targets
  • The system’s core anti-forgetting function
“I don’t think I’ll ever forget that Sicily was dominated by Arabs around the year 1000, because those are gists linked to strong anchors. But all of the different names, sequences, when was Hiero the First, when was Archimedes — it’s all going to be a mush.”
Design implication

The system already distinguishes chapter_recall vs. curriculum recall at the UI level. This distinction should propagate to storage and downstream use: distant recall feeds salience scoring and personalization; fresh recall feeds retention targeting.

Section IV

The “Digital Twin” Gap

What a digital twin would know

  • “Stian finds the Alexander-Aristotle relationship fascinating”
  • “He consistently connects Macedonian politics to Athenian intellectual life”
  • “He confuses the chronology of Hiero I and Archimedes”
  • “His mental model of Byzantine history is organized around Constantinople as a city, not around emperors”
  • “When thinking about Aristotle, he naturally reaches for French neoclassical drama connections”
  • “He has strong knowledge of Arab Sicily but weak on Norman succession”

What the system currently knows

knowledge_state: "engaged"
confidence: 0.45
last_score: "partly"
review_count: 3

That’s the entire user model for a topic. The rich signal from voice elicitation is reduced to these fields.

Section V

What the Research Says

Recent research from knowledge-systems-deep-dive.md, knowledge-assessment-research.md, beyond-flashcards-knowledge-retention.md, and academic-landscape-and-collaborations.md provides deep foundations. Here are the findings organized by what they tell us to build.

A. Knowledge structure > knowledge amount

Expert vs. Novice Knowledge Structures Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981) — Cognitive Science
Experts categorize by deep principles; novices by surface features. The difference isn’t knowledge amount — it’s organization. Experts have richly interconnected schemas organized around core principles; novices store disconnected facts. Schneider, Korkel & Weinert (1989) confirmed: low-IQ soccer experts outperformed high-IQ soccer novices on recall. Knowledge structure trumps general ability.
Actionable → Track not just what nodes the user knows but how they connect them. Voice transcripts reveal this: do recalled items cluster by theme/period (expert) or list randomly (novice)?
Pathfinder Networks: The North Star Metric Goldsmith, Johnson & Acton (1991) — J. Educational Psychology
Similarity between a student’s Pathfinder network and the expert’s network correlates with exam performance at r = .74. The curriculum graph IS the expert network. The gap between the learner’s demonstrated structure and the curriculum graph is the single most informative metric of learning. Three metrics: PRX (proximity), GTD (graph-theoretic distance), PFC (Pathfinder closeness).
Actionable → Derive the learner network from: (1) which nodes they mention in voice, (2) which connections they articulate, (3) which nodes they demonstrate understanding of in reviews. Compare to curriculum graph using PFC/GTD. This distance IS the learning metric.
SNAFU: Semantic Networks from Free Recall Zemla & Austerweil — Memory & Cognition (2026)
Python tool (AusterweilLab/snafu-py, v2.6.7) for estimating semantic networks from free recall. Six methods including U-INVITE (maximum likelihood censored random walk). Adaptation needed: extract entities/concepts from voice transcripts, treat as recall “items” in order, estimate network structure.
Actionable → pip install git+https://github.com/AusterweilLab/snafu-py. Extract named entities from transcripts in recall order, run U-INVITE, compare monthly against curriculum graph.

B. Free recall is superior to flashcards

The Retrieval Hierarchy Karpicke & Blunt (2011, Science) + Carpenter (2009)
Free recall promotes both relational AND item-specific processing. Cued recall promotes only item-specific. Recognition (multiple choice) promotes only familiarity. Free recall “enhances relational processing — the kind that supports conceptual understanding.” Crucially: elaborative retrieval (reconstructing knowledge from context) strengthens the entire semantic network around a concept, not just the target. Testing concept A can strengthen memory for related, untested concept B — but only if they’re well-integrated.
Actionable → Voice elicitation (free recall) is the superior assessment AND learning mechanism. Quiz cards (cued recall) are secondary. The connected curriculum structure means reviewing one node strengthens related nodes through spreading activation. This validates investing more in voice data.
Voice Sweep Protocol Proposed in knowledge-systems-deep-dive.md §4.2
A domain-wide voice assessment (distinct from per-node elicitation): “Tell me the history of Sicily — everything you remember, in roughly chronological order. Speak for about 3 minutes.” Repeated monthly, this measures growth on five dimensions: Coverage (fraction of nodes mentioned), Depth (average knowledge level), Connectivity (connections articulated), Accuracy (FActScore-style claim verification), Organization (expert-like clustering vs. random).
Actionable → Implement monthly voice sweeps as a longitudinal growth metric. Same prompt repeated = comparable over time. Scoring pipeline: transcribe → extract entities in order → map to curriculum nodes → compute coverage, accuracy, clustering → compare to expert graph.

C. Different knowledge types decay differently

Fuzzy-Trace Theory: Verbatim vs. Gist Brainerd & Reyna + MINERVA2 integration (Chang et al., 2025)
Two parallel memory traces encoded for every experience: verbatim (exact facts, dates, names) decays within days; gist (frameworks, arguments, “so what”) persists weeks-to-months. Without reinforcement: 1 week = recall main arguments + key examples; 1 month = thesis clear, arguments fuzzy, examples gone; 3 months = only the “big idea”; 1 year = vague sense of “having read something about X.” A single well-timed reconnection can restore the full gist framework.
Actionable → Type-differentiated scheduling: dates/names start at 1 day, grow slowly (×2.0); connections/frameworks start at 3 days, grow fast (×3.0). The research proposes concrete multipliers in knowledge-systems-deep-dive.md §3.2. Also: Duolingo’s Half-Life Regression (log(h) = θ · x) can train per-type decay rates directly on Petrarca’s review data.
Successive Relearning Rawson & Dunlosky (2022) + Karpicke
Three spaced relearning sessions may be sufficient for maximal benefit. Recalling items once across 3 spaced sessions beats recalling each item 3 times in one session (by 2×). Retention at 1 month: 68% with successive relearning vs. 11% baseline. Extra effort on initial criterion doesn’t persist — additional spaced encounters matter more than deep drilling on first contact.
Actionable → Don’t over-drill a concept in one session. Three encounters across different sources (book + article + voice recall) is the sweet spot. Validate with Petrarca’s data: do nodes encountered in 3+ contexts retain better?

D. The brain already connects — the system should help

Knowledge Resonance & Schema Theory mPFC research (npj Science of Learning, 2018)
The medial prefrontal cortex functions as a “resonance detector” that recognizes when new information fits existing schemas. When congruence is detected: (1) mPFC signals a match, (2) new information integrates into the existing structure, (3) the old schema is strengthened and updated simultaneously. Reactivation of previously learned information during new learning leads to integration of old and new memories.
Actionable → When the user reads an article mentioning Arab Sicily, surface what they said about it in voice elicitation as a margin annotation. This triggers the resonance detector. The system provides the cue; the brain does the integration.
Natural Spacing > Artificial SRS Karpicke’s Episodic Context Account (2014) + Bjork
Reading new material that touches on previously-read concepts creates “natural spacing” superior to flashcard SRS: novel context (more retrieval cues), incidental retrieval (recognition triggers recall), elaborative connection (reader compares perspectives), intrinsic motivation (new article is interesting, not a chore). Transfer-appropriate processing: the best retrieval cue for something encoded during reading is another reading context, not a flashcard.
Actionable → Give a stability bump (×1.3) when an article maps to a curriculum node, even without explicit review. Track whether article-reinforced nodes show higher retention than review-only nodes. Proposed in knowledge-systems-deep-dive.md §3.3.
Evolving Summaries beyond-flashcards-knowledge-retention.md §8.2
Your summary of a book should grow as you read related material. After initial reading, generate a “current understanding” summary. As related articles are encountered, regenerate: “Your understanding of Pirenne has been enriched by 3 articles this month. New connections: [X challenged the dating], [Y provided supporting evidence].” Each revision requires elaborative retrieval. Makes the value of continued reading visible.
Actionable → This is Proposal E with a concrete mechanism. Regenerate per-domain summaries after every 3 new elicitations or article reads. These become the primary context for all LLM calls in that domain.

E. How to analyze voice transcripts computationally

Prompt Effectiveness Hierarchy Elaborative interrogation research (beyond-flashcards §5)
Research-ranked prompt types: (1) Connection “How does this relate to [other book concept]?” — best. (2) Explanation “Why does the author argue X rather than Y?” (3) Implication “What would follow if correct?” (4) Contrast. (5) Application. (6) Recognition (multiple choice) — worst. Current review cards use a mix, but should prefer the top of this hierarchy. Connection prompts require knowing what other things the learner knows — which requires the learner profile.
Actionable → Inject learner profile into review prompts specifically to enable connection-style questions: “The learner knows X from [Book A] — ask how this relates.” This is the highest-value prompt type and it’s impossible without user knowledge context.
Coh-Metrix: Transcript Quality Scoring McNamara et al. (2004) + Kim et al. (2026)
200+ computational measures of cohesion and coherence, applicable to transcripts: referential cohesion (do successive sentences share concepts?), deep cohesion (are causal/logical connectives present?), LSA overlap (semantic relatedness of adjacent statements), lexical diversity (vocabulary breadth = richer representation). Kim et al. (2026) showed NLP can score scientific explanations while “eliminating linguistic discrimination” — assessing content quality not language proficiency.
Actionable → After transcription, compute basic cohesion metrics: count causal connectives (“because”, “therefore”, “which led to”), measure entity co-reference chains, track lexical diversity. These are cheap to compute and provide a “structural quality” score per elicitation.
FActScore-style Claim Verification knowledge-systems-deep-dive.md §4.1
Decompose transcript into atomic claims, verify each against curriculum definitions and key_facts. Percentage of supported claims = factual accuracy score. Combined with the Pathfinder organization score and voice sweep coverage, this gives three independent dimensions of knowledge quality.
Actionable → Already partially implemented via the captured/missed extraction in VOICE_ELICITATION_PROMPT. Make it more rigorous: ask the LLM to decompose into atomic claims with confidence, then verify against key_facts database.

F. Historical thinking is its own dimension

Historical Thinking Frameworks Wineburg (2001), Seixas “Big Six” (2013)
Historical understanding isn’t just knowing facts. Wineburg’s four strategies: sourcing (who created this? what’s their perspective?), contextualization (when/where? what else was happening?), corroboration (compare accounts), close reading (what claims does the text actually make?). Seixas adds: historical significance, continuity and change, cause and consequence, historical perspectives, ethical dimensions. Each has graduated benchmarks from naive to sophisticated.
Actionable → Voice transcripts reveal which thinking skills the learner employs naturally. “I wonder if Athens willingly joined Alexander” = perspective-taking. “This led to the Baroque rebuilding” = cause/consequence. Tag these in the learner profile. Over time, you can track whether the learner develops more sophisticated historical thinking.
Retrieval-Induced Facilitation (not just forgetting) Chan, McDermott & Roediger (2006)
Retrieving some items can impair recall of related items (Anderson et al., 1994). But this reverses for well-integrated knowledge with delay: testing actually strengthens untested related items if they share a coherent mental model. “High integration + delay = facilitation rather than forgetting.” The connected curriculum structure provides natural protection.
Actionable → The system should maintain broad coverage across a domain rather than deep-drilling a small subset. Current round-robin interleaving already does this. But voice elicitation across multiple nodes accelerates the integration that protects against retrieval-induced forgetting.
GENCAT + Matuschak’s Salience Feng & Lan (UMass, Feb 2026) + Matuschak (2024–26)
GENCAT uses LLMs to predict student open-ended responses conditioned on their knowledge state, then compares to actual. 4.3% AUC improvement. Closest academic system to Petrarca’s voice assessment. Matuschak distinguishes retrieval prompts (test recall) from salience prompts (keep ideas top-of-mind for application). Standard SRS scheduling is “probably all wrong” for salience. Framework nodes (“causes of Roman decline”) should activate when reading new material, not just be recallable on demand.
Actionable → Two concrete ideas: (1) Use learner profiles to predict what the user should say about a topic, then compare to what they actually say — the delta is the learning target. (2) Separate salience items from retrieval items in scheduling: frameworks get longer intervals, resurfaced during reading contexts rather than quiz contexts.
Section VI

Five Proposals

D
Inject existing voice data into review prompts
High impact Very low effort

The simplest possible improvement. When generating a review question for node X, query voice_transcripts for the most recent elicitation and append the existing captured, missed, interesting, and wonderings fields to the prompt.

Implementation: In generate_question() (review_engine.py:1315), add a query: SELECT llm_result FROM voice_transcripts WHERE node_id = ? AND source = 'elicitation' ORDER BY created_at DESC LIMIT 1. Parse the JSON, format as “LEARNER CONTEXT” section, append to prompt. ~300-500 extra tokens.

What changes: The review question can now target known gaps, build on connections the learner already made, and avoid re-testing what they clearly know. Zero new infrastructure — just a DB query and string formatting.

A
Learner Knowledge Profile per node
Very high impact Medium effort

After each voice elicitation, compile a structured “learner knowledge profile” that accumulates across elicitations. Not a replacement — a merge. Knowledge grows.

Schema:
node_id, domain_id
facts_known: [“Aristotle was student of Plato”, “Tutored Alexander”, ...]
facts_missed: [“Invented formal logic”, ...]
misconceptions: [“Father was tutor to Philip (actually physician to Amyntas III)”]
connections_made: [“Alexander-Aristotle personal relationship”, ...]
interests: [“French neoclassical drama reception”]
organizing_framework: “Biographical arc + political context”
thinking_skills: [“perspective-taking”, “contextualization”]

Research connection: This is the Petrarca equivalent of Pathfinder’s learner network. Each profile entry represents a node in the learner’s actual semantic network, with its connections and weights.

Cost: One Gemini Flash call per elicitation to merge new data into existing profile. ~200-500 tokens stored per node.

B
Cross-topic knowledge retrieval (RAG)
Very high impact Medium effort

When generating content about a topic, retrieve relevant snippets from all the user’s voice data — not just the current node.

Example: When generating a review card about “The Lyceum,” pull in what the user said about Plato (Aristotle’s teacher), Alexander (his student), Athens (its location), Arab transmission of Greek philosophy (its legacy).

Implementation: Use limbic.amygdala embeddings on transcript chunks (paragraph-level). Store in transcript_embeddings table. At review time, embed the node description, cosine-search top-k passages, include as “what the learner has said about related topics.”

Research connection: This is how SNAFU’s semantic network estimation works — by analyzing how concepts cluster across free recall episodes. The embedding similarity IS the semantic distance.

C
Entity-level knowledge aggregation
High impact Medium effort

For each entity (person, place, event) mentioned across all voice data, build an aggregated “what the user knows about X.”

Example for “Alexander the Great”:

  • From Aristotle elicitation: “Tutored by Aristotle, sent specimens back, destroyed Stagira”
  • From Sicily elicitation: “Part of the Hellenistic world after Alexander”
  • From Persian Empire (future): “Defeated Darius, spread Greek culture east”
Research connection: The entity-profiles-design.md already specifies a 4-layer scaffold: identity → context → connections → significance. Voice data populates all four layers with the user’s actual knowledge, not just curriculum definitions. The overlapping curricula vision means entity knowledge should be tracked per lens (Alexander-the-general vs. Alexander-the-patron-of-learning).
E
Domain knowledge summaries (“the digital twin”)
Very high impact Low effort (once A+B exist)

Periodically compile ALL voice data for a domain into a 1–2 page “learner knowledge portrait.” This becomes the primary context for all content generation in that domain.

Instead of passing raw node descriptions, pass: “Here’s what this specific learner knows, thinks, and cares about in Ancient Greece.”

Implementation: One Opus call per domain, run after every 3 new elicitations. Store as document. Include: knowledge map (what’s strong, what’s weak), organizing frameworks, cross-domain connections the learner makes, open questions, interests, misconceptions to address.

This is the actual digital twin — a document that represents you. It can be inspected, edited, and shared. It grows richer with every voice interaction.

The path

  1. Proposal D — immediate win. Query existing voice_transcripts.llm_result and inject into prompts. Can be done in half a session. Proves the concept.
  2. Proposal A — the foundation. Structured profiles that accumulate. This is what all other proposals build on.
  3. Proposal B — the connections. limbic embeddings on transcripts. Enables “what has the user said about topics related to X?”
  4. C + E emerge naturally from the infrastructure built in A + B.

Elicitation prompt enhancements (all proposals)

The VOICE_ELICITATION_PROMPT should also be enriched to extract more structured data:

  • Confidence tagging: “I’m pretty sure” vs. “I wonder if” — tag each fact with user’s apparent certainty
  • Framework detection: “What organizing principle does the learner use?” (chronological, biographical, geographic)
  • Connection classification: For each “interesting” item: temporal, causal, cross-domain, personal interpretation
  • Thinking skills: Sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, perspective-taking (per Seixas/Wineburg)
Section VIII

Open Questions

When should profiles be regenerated?

After every elicitation (incremental merge), or batched? Incremental is more responsive but risks drift from accumulation errors. A periodic full recompile (every 3 elicitations) might be more reliable.

Pre-existing vs. app-learned knowledge

Should the system distinguish “I knew this before Petrarca” from “I learned this through the app”? Distant recall of pre-existing knowledge is different from demonstrating retention of app-presented material. Both are valuable but for different purposes.

Filter bubble risk

If the system knows the user finds X interesting, should it surface more X-related content? This creates a filter bubble. Counter: the curriculum structure already provides breadth; personalization only affects how content is presented, not what is shown.

Voice notes integration

Open-ended voice notes (not tied to curriculum nodes) contain knowledge too. Should they feed the same profile system? They’re harder to route to specific nodes but may contain the most interesting cross-domain connections.

Measurement

How do we know if richer prompts produce better review cards? The publishable-experiments-research.md outlines N-of-1 methodology: A/B test enriched vs. standard prompts, measure retention at 2-week and 4-week intervals using Tau-U effect sizes. The voice elicitation itself is the measurement instrument — does knowledge structure improve faster with personalized cards?
