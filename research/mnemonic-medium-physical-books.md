# Mnemonic Medium & Timeful Texts Applied to Physical Books

**Date:** 2026-03-15
**Purpose:** Deep research into Andy Matuschak's work and the mnemonic medium / timeful text vision, applied to Petrarca's physical book companion feature.

---

## 1. Andy Matuschak's Research Arc (2019–2025)

### The Original Thesis: "Why Books Don't Work" (2019)

Matuschak's foundational argument: books and lectures share an implicit (and false) theory of learning — transmissionism. The reader's eyes "skip like stones across the surface of the page. They never processed those words beyond visual decoding." The problem isn't that readers forget — it's that **they never understood in the first place**, though they feel like they did.

Source: [Why books don't work](https://andymatuschak.org/books/)

### Quantum Country: The Mnemonic Medium Experiment (2019–2022)

Matuschak and Michael Nielsen embedded spaced repetition prompts directly into a web-based quantum computing textbook. Key results:

- **After 30 min of practice**: most readers recall answers to nearly all 112 questions across intervals of 2+ weeks
- **After 1 hour**: retention extends to 5+ weeks
- **After 1.5 hours**: retention extends to 9+ weeks
- **Top quartile**: 3 months of retention for ~1 hour of practice
- **25th percentile**: 1 month of retention for 1.5 hours of practice
- **Median card**: 82–93% recall at 4–6 week intervals

The data showed **exponential returns** on practice time, though Matuschak cautioned that the exponential shape partly reflects the scheduler itself (which doubles/halves intervals). The measured retention is a **lower bound** — true retention intervals are likely higher.

**Critical limitation**: The forgetting curves were "suspiciously flat" after the first few repetitions, suggesting the scheduler was too conservative and under-testing readers.

Sources: [Quantum Country](https://quantum.country/qcvc), [Exponentials and forgetting in Quantum Country](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z93QR51f6HAUPLVDxE6KT1T), [Log: Quantum Country analysis](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z9bhN2NXqM4HYQQBtM1rMXX)

### The Pivot: Comprehension, Not Memory (2023)

Matuschak's most important insight came from working closely with a student named Alex on physics:

> "What seems like a problem of forgetting is sometimes a problem of reading comprehension — never having understood in the first place — and we can't reliably tell the difference."

He observed that **even highly paid knowledge workers routinely fail at basic reading comprehension**. In his own University of Chicago Great Books course, at least one facilitator question per session reveals he "simply hadn't comprehended what the text said in some important passage — and hadn't noticed."

This reframing is crucial: **when memory failures are actually comprehension failures in disguise, memory augmentation is the wrong solution.**

Sources: [Reading comprehension and memory systems](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zLButXjJvGCpWKHzqhXEhhm), [Highlight-driven practice and comprehension support](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zPNoWjs5jQ7iMDpxxBtjSbg)

### "Fluid Practice for Fluid Understanding" (May 2023)

Working with Alex, Matuschak discovered that "mere" retrieval practice of conceptual material produces **brittle understanding** which transfers poorly. Immediately after reading, what Alex most needed was **reading comprehension checks** — he hadn't actually absorbed important statements on the page.

Problems identified with the mnemonic medium as originally conceived:
1. **Feels authoritarian** — author dictates what matters
2. **Comprehension gaps are routine** — prompts assume understanding that doesn't exist
3. **Practicing others' prompts doesn't work well with conceptual material** — the act of writing prompts is itself the learning
4. **Retrieval practice of conceptual material produces brittle understanding**

Source: [Fluid practice for fluid understanding](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z21VhT726p7bX8JcGM41QSA)

### Highlight-Driven Prototype (Sept–Nov 2023)

Matuschak built an augmented reading environment centered on **highlighting as the core interaction**:

- A **"purple highlighter"** that automatically generates spaced repetition prompts about highlighted ideas
- **"Suggested highlights"** at the end of each section — important phrases the author flagged that the reader missed (comprehension check)
- The reader can fluidly move between consuming, editing, refactoring, and writing prompts

**Results from 14 participants:**
- Readers "broadly loved" the augmented highlighting concept
- Most already highlighted but admitted it didn't affect how well they learned — highlighting was described as "fidgeting behavior" and "ad-hoc bookmarking"
- Most were "extremely happy" with the auto-generated prompts: *"The prompts captured my own intent to the point where it took me some time to realize that they weren't written by me"*

Source: [Initial results from highlight-driven prototype](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z9V2PxHVYB9p5DeCnQXcfJa)

### The Great Books Problem (Oct 2023)

A devastating self-observation:

> "I'm now in my second year of U Chicago's 'Great Books' course, and I notice: I don't know how to write useful spaced repetition prompts around what I'm learning. It's not details of one book that I care about, but the habits and mindsets that I'm developing. Hard to externalize."

This reveals a fundamental limitation: for deep humanistic reading — exactly the kind Petrarca supports — **the valuable outcomes resist prompt-ification.** What matters is developing intellectual habits, ways of seeing, interpretive frameworks — not recalling facts.

Source: [Tweet](https://x.com/andy_matuschak/status/1711501772790063224)

### BookBridge and Latticework (Late 2023–2024)

Two collaborative prototypes:

**BookBridge** (with Derrek Chow, Dec 2023): An experiment augmenting the book reading experience with dynamic media. Demo available to Patreon supporters.

**Latticework** (with Matthew Siu, Aug 2024): Unifies annotation and freeform text editing for augmented sensemaking. Released as an Obsidian plugin. Key insight: annotation tools feel great but don't support follow-up thinking; text editors give flexibility but are disorienting; **unifying both** could offer the best of both worlds.

Sources: [BookBridge demo](https://www.patreon.com/posts/demo-early-look-95131117), [Latticework](https://www.matthewsiu.com/Latticework)

### Current Status (2025)

Matuschak's public output has slowed in 2025 (personal circumstances — his mother's illness). His most recent substantial public post is "Preparatory notes for a manifesto" (May 2025). His stated focus remains "an augmented book which actively helps people understand, remember, and use what they read."

On Twitter he's been posting about attention management (scrolling-free days enable deeper reading), and appreciating HCI research in creative domains (EuterPen multimodal composition system).

## 2. The Timeful Text Concept

Matuschak and Nielsen's concept of "timeful texts" describes texts with affordances extending beyond the initial reading experience — **texts that continue the conversation with the reader over weeks and months.**

The core problem: physical books are "stuck on the page, generally read linearly and continuously in a few sittings." They have **no direct means of communicating with readers over time.** A book doesn't reach out to you three weeks later to ask whether you've applied its ideas, or to remind you of a passage that connects to something you encountered yesterday.

Source: [Timeful Texts](https://numinous.productions/timeful/)

## 3. Michael Nielsen's Contributions

### "Augmenting Long-term Memory" (2018)

Nielsen's seminal essay on his years of daily Anki use. Key principles:

1. **Make your own cards** — users report poor results with cards made by others
2. **Build questions on top of other cards** — SRS can build understanding of almost anything, not just facts
3. **Connect to meaningful projects** — Anki works best when you collect knowledge toward a real goal
4. **Beware of committing false information** — add attribution when uncertain

### "How to Make Memory Systems Widespread?" (2020)

Nielsen's crucial reframing: **the main barrier isn't better tools or UI — it's culturally developing more powerful and transferable memory practices.**

He compares memory systems to practices like yoga, meditation, surfing:
- These practices took **centuries of refinement**
- You can watch an expert surfer and imitate them, but **you can't watch someone do memory practice** — the skill is internal
- Memory systems aren't ends in themselves (like dance or basketball) — they serve **some other purpose**

Nielsen suspects **hundreds of millions of people** could benefit from skillful memory practice — it's closer to reading and writing than to surfing.

Sources: [Augmenting Long-term Memory](https://augmentingcognition.com/), [How to make memory systems widespread?](https://michaelnotebook.com/mmsw/)

## 4. Beyond Flashcards: Creative Uses of Spaced Repetition

### Programming Attention

Matuschak's note "Spaced repetition systems can be used to program attention" reframes SRS not as a memory tool but as an **attention allocation system**. When you outsource scheduling decisions, you make a coarse choice ("devote 10 minutes to SRS") and let the machine direct your attention within that block.

Source: [SRS can program attention](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Spaced_repetition_systems_can_be_used_to_program_attention)

### Developing Inklings

SRS can support **incremental thinking** — returning to hundreds of half-formed ideas over time, considering only a few per day. "What's often needed is marination: let's come back in a few days, see what bubbles up."

Source: [Incrementally develop inklings](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Spaced_repetition_may_be_a_helpful_tool_to_incrementally_develop_inklings)

### Prompting Application, Synthesis, and Creation

Beyond memorization, SRS cards can prompt you to **apply** a concept to your own life, **synthesize** across multiple ideas, or **create** something new. These "unorthodox cards" go far beyond Q&A.

Source: [SRS for application, synthesis, creation](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Spaced_repetition_memory_systems_can_be_used_to_prompt_application,_synthesis,_and_creation)

### Developing Habits

SRS can prompt **behavior change** — not "what is X?" but "have you done X today?" or "next time you encounter Y, try Z."

Source: [SRS for habits](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Spaced_repetition_may_be_a_helpful_tool_to_develop_or_change_habits)

## 5. Desirable Difficulties Research

### Core Framework (Bjork, 1994)

Robert and Elizabeth Bjork identified four **desirable difficulties** — mechanisms that make learning harder during practice but produce better long-term retention:

1. **Spacing** — distributing practice over time (10–30% better retention in meta-analyses)
2. **Interleaving** — mixing different types of problems/topics
3. **Retrieval practice** — testing yourself rather than re-reading
4. **Generation** — producing answers rather than recognizing them

### The Generation Effect

Self-generated information is remembered far better than read information because generation activates **multiple cognitive processes**: semantic elaboration, distinctive processing, effortful retrieval. When you generate, you search memory, make connections, and construct responses — creating more retrieval pathways than passive reading.

Sources: [Desirable Difficulties - Bjork Lab](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/research/), [Generation Effect](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/generation-effect-active-learning)

### The Testing Effect

Long-term memory is increased when learning time includes **retrieval practice**. Re-reading creates an **illusion of competence** — the words feel familiar on second read, but this doesn't mean the material is understood or retained.

Source: [Testing Effect - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testing_effect)

### Elaborative Interrogation

Asking "Why is this true?" or "Why does it make sense that...?" forces deeper processing than simply reading. The **self-explanation effect** (explaining material to yourself) outperforms both repetition and elaborative interrogation alone.

Source: [Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/)

### Metacognitive Illusions

People consistently **believe** massed repetition is more effective than spaced repetition. This gap between what feels effective and what actually works is a core challenge for any system trying to implement desirable difficulties.

## 6. The LLM Question: Auto-Generated vs. Human-Written Prompts

Matuschak notes that LLMs lack **prompt-writing patterns for complex conceptual material.** The problem isn't that LLMs generate "bad" prompts — it's that the resulting prompts reinforce the surface: **what is said, rather than what it means or why it matters.**

LLMs can generate elaborated prompts with significant guidance (e.g., "generate an example and ask whether it's in echelon form" rather than just "make prompts about this concept"). But this requires the prompt author to already have deep understanding — defeating the purpose.

For **simple declarative knowledge**, GPT-4 class models can produce usable prompts. For **conceptual understanding, synthesis, and application**, they need substantial steering and remain best used as a starting point for manual editing.

Source: [LLMs lack prompt-writing patterns](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zGkLPdiEs7Qohkesq7TNiBe)

## 7. Existing Approaches to Physical Book → Digital Review

### Readwise
- Camera-based OCR scanning for physical book highlights
- Daily email review with spaced repetition (decay-based half-life algorithm)
- Two-part review: unprocessed highlights (random) + Mastery cards (spaced repetition with active recall)
- Can convert highlights into "Mastery" cards — fill-in-the-blank / cloze deletion
- Initial half-lives: 7 days ("soon"), 14 days ("later"), 28 days ("someday")

Source: [Readwise](https://readwise.io/), [Adding Intention to Spaced Repetition](https://blog.readwise.io/adding-intention-to-spaced-repetition/)

### Screvi
- Scan physical book pages with camera, highlight with finger
- Voice notes converted to highlights via transcription
- Daily review based on spaced repetition
- Feed-based interface pulling from multiple sources

### Ollie Lovell's Workflow (Dendro)
- Physical marking: arrows for engaging text, stars in margins, page corner marks
- Two bookmarks: "Read to here" + "Dendro from here"
- Creates empty Dendro note per book, transfers marked passages
- Integration of physical reading with digital spaced repetition

Source: [How I apply spaced repetition to hard copy books](https://www.ollielovell.com/how-i-apply-spaced-repetition-to-hard-copy-books/)

## 8. Synthesis: What This Means for Petrarca's Book Companion

### The Core Tension

Petrarca's physical book companion captures three types of input: **page photos** (OCR'd), **voice notes** (transcribed), and **text notes**. The question is: how to transform these raw captures into something that keeps the book's ideas alive over time — without becoming a flashcard app.

The research points to several critical insights:

1. **The problem is comprehension, not memory.** Most "forgetting" is actually never-having-understood. Any resurfacing system must address comprehension first.

2. **Highlighting is fidgeting, not learning.** People love highlighting but it doesn't improve retention. The value isn't in the highlight itself — it's in what happens after.

3. **Self-generated material is dramatically more effective than authored prompts.** Voice notes and handwritten text captures already embody the generation effect — the user constructed them.

4. **Habits and mindsets resist prompt-ification.** For humanistic/literary reading, the valuable outcomes aren't facts but ways of thinking. Traditional SRS fails here.

5. **Attention programming > memory drilling.** The most promising use of spaced resurfacing is directing attention — "think about this again" — rather than testing recall.

6. **Marination beats memorization.** For developing ideas, what's needed is periodic re-encounter — "let's come back in a few days, see what bubbles up."

## 9. Concrete Experimental Directions for Petrarca

### Experiment 1: "Resonance Resurfacing" — Not Testing, Re-encountering

**What to build:** A daily or periodic "resurfacing" screen that shows 3–5 captures from books at expanding intervals. NOT as flashcards to test recall, but as **re-encounters** to prompt reflection.

**How it works:**
- Each capture appears with its original context (book, chapter, page)
- Below the capture, show an LLM-generated **"resonance prompt"** — not "what did the author say?" but:
  - "How does this connect to something you've been thinking about recently?"
  - "Has your view on this changed since you first captured it?"
  - "Where have you encountered this idea since reading it?"
  - "What would you add to this note now?"
- User can: **dismiss** (schedule further out), **respond** (voice/text — creates a new linked capture), or **connect** (link to another capture or article)
- Interval scheduling uses Readwise-style decay (half-life based), but triggered by **engagement quality** not recall accuracy

**Why this might work:** It combines the spacing effect with the generation effect. The user isn't recalling facts — they're generating new connections. It respects Matuschak's finding that habits/mindsets resist prompting by asking **open-ended, generative questions** rather than closed recall questions.

**What to measure:** Do users respond? Do responses get richer over time? Do users report feeling more connected to books they've finished?

### Experiment 2: "Comprehension Audit" — Post-Capture Reflection

**What to build:** Immediately after a capture session (photo/voice/text), present 1–2 **comprehension check questions** generated by an LLM from the captured content.

**How it works:**
- User photographs a page or records a voice note
- After OCR/transcription, the LLM generates a question like:
  - "The author claims X leads to Y. What's the mechanism they propose?"
  - "You noted this passage. Can you explain the key distinction being made, in your own words?"
- User responds (voice or text)
- The response is stored alongside the original capture as an **"annotation layer"**

**Why this might work:** Addresses Matuschak's core finding that the problem is comprehension, not memory. The immediate-post-capture moment is when the book is still in the reader's hands — they can flip back and re-read. This is the "reading comprehension check" that Matuschak found Alex most needed.

**What to measure:** Do users actually respond? Does responding correlate with richer captures over time? Do users who respond show better recall of the material weeks later?

### Experiment 3: "Chapter Digest" — Synthesis Prompts at Chapter Boundaries

**What to build:** When a user updates their reading position to a new chapter, generate a **synthesis prompt** from all captures in the completed chapter.

**How it works:**
- User finishes Chapter 7, updates position to Chapter 8
- System presents: "You made 4 captures in Chapter 7. Here they are together. In 2–3 sentences, what was the chapter really about?"
- User responds (voice is ideal — lower friction, more elaboration)
- The synthesis becomes a new capture type: **"chapter synthesis"**
- During resurfacing, chapter syntheses are shown alongside their source captures

**Why this might work:** Forces the generation effect at a meaningful boundary. The elaborative interrogation literature shows "why?" and "what does this mean?" prompts drive deeper processing. A chapter boundary is a natural reflection point that doesn't interrupt reading flow.

### Experiment 4: "Cross-Book Threads" — Connecting Ideas Across Books

**What to build:** Use embeddings to find semantic connections between captures from **different books**. Surface these connections during resurfacing.

**How it works:**
- Embed all captures (OCR text, transcripts, text notes) using Nomic embeddings (already in the pipeline)
- During resurfacing, when showing a capture from Book A, also show: "Related capture from Book B" (above cosine threshold)
- Prompt: "You captured similar ideas in [Book A] and [Book B]. How do these two authors' perspectives differ?"
- Can also connect book captures to **article claims** already in the knowledge index

**Why this might work:** This is Matuschak's "connecting and relating ideas" principle. Petrarca already has the embedding infrastructure. Cross-book connections are exactly what humanist readers value — it's what the Great Books program trains. The system does the "noticing the connection" work that the reader's memory can't reliably do.

### Experiment 5: "The Wizard-of-Oz Highlighter" for Physical Books

**What to build:** When a user photographs a page, use LLM vision to identify which passages are highlighted/underlined/annotated, then generate prompts specifically about **what the reader chose to mark**.

**How it works:**
- User photographs a page with their own markings
- Vision model (Gemini, already integrated via `call_vision()`) identifies marked passages vs. unmarked text
- For marked passages: generate an elaborative prompt ("Why did this strike you?")
- For unmarked passages that seem important: generate a **"suggested highlight"** — "The author makes a key point here about X that you might have skipped. What do you think?"
- This directly adapts Matuschak's highlight-driven prototype to physical books

**Why this might work:** It's Matuschak's most successful prototype concept (readers loved it) applied to the physical book domain. The "suggested highlight" feature addresses the comprehension problem — catching ideas the reader's eyes skipped over.

### Experiment 6: "Incremental Rethinking" — Open Prompts on Expanding Schedules

**What to build:** For each book, maintain a small set (3–5) of **open-ended "rethinking" prompts** that evolve over time.

**How it works:**
- When a book is first added, generate 3 seed prompts from the book's description/TOC:
  - "What is your current understanding of [topic X]?"
  - "What do you already believe about [question Y]?"
- As the user reads and captures, prompts evolve based on what they've engaged with
- On a weekly/biweekly schedule, present one prompt — but **the expected answer changes over time**:
  - Week 1: "What's your current take on [idea from Ch. 3]?"
  - Week 4: "Has your view of [idea from Ch. 3] changed since you read it?"
  - Week 12: "Looking back, what was the most lasting idea from [Book]?"
- Responses are stored as a **"reading journal"** — a timestamped sequence showing how the user's thinking evolved

**Why this might work:** This directly addresses the Great Books problem Matuschak identified. It doesn't try to test recall of specific facts — it prompts the reader to notice their own intellectual development. The open-ended nature supports habits/mindsets rather than facts. The journal format creates a valuable artifact: a record of intellectual growth.

### Experiment 7: "Conversational Review" — LLM as Interlocutor

**What to build:** Instead of traditional resurfacing (show card → recall), use an LLM to have a **brief conversation** about resurfaced captures.

**How it works:**
- System selects captures for resurfacing (spacing algorithm)
- Instead of showing the capture directly, the LLM opens with a question that requires the user to recall and elaborate:
  - "Three weeks ago you were reading [Book]. You captured a passage about [topic]. What do you remember about the author's argument?"
- Based on the user's response, the LLM either:
  - Shows the original capture and asks a follow-up ("How does this connect to [other thing]?")
  - Probes deeper if the user remembers well ("What's an example from your own life?")
  - Gently corrects if the user misremembers ("Actually, the author argued the opposite — that X leads to Y, not the reverse. Why do you think they took that position?")
- 2–3 turns maximum — this is a micro-conversation, not a tutoring session

**Why this might work:** This is "conversational spaced repetition" (David Bieber's concept). It combines retrieval practice (user must generate from memory), elaborative interrogation (follow-up questions), and the testing effect — all in a format that feels like talking about books with a thoughtful friend, not doing flashcards.

## 10. Recommended Starting Point

**Start with Experiment 1 (Resonance Resurfacing) + Experiment 5 (Wizard-of-Oz Highlighter).**

Rationale:
- Experiment 1 is the minimal viable "timeful" extension — it makes captures come alive over time with minimal engineering (scheduling + LLM prompt generation)
- Experiment 5 leverages existing infrastructure (`call_vision()` on the server) and directly adapts Matuschak's most successful prototype to physical books
- Both can be built incrementally and tested with real use
- They address different moments: Experiment 5 enriches the **capture** experience, Experiment 1 enriches the **review** experience
- Neither requires the user to change their reading habits — they work with natural behaviors (photographing pages, revisiting notes)

**What NOT to build:**
- Traditional flashcards / Q&A pairs from book captures — this is exactly the approach Matuschak has spent 5 years moving away from
- Automated prompt generation without user involvement — LLMs produce surface-level prompts for conceptual material
- Gamification / streak mechanics — these optimize for compliance, not understanding
- Complex scheduling algorithms initially — start with simple expanding intervals and tune based on usage data

## 11. Key Open Questions

1. **Voice vs. text responses during resurfacing**: Voice is lower-friction and encourages elaboration, but harder to analyze and connect. Should resurfacing prompts prefer voice responses?

2. **How much LLM involvement?** Matuschak's research suggests the act of writing prompts IS the learning. But for physical books, the capture-to-prompt gap is large. LLMs could bridge this — but at what cost to learning?

3. **Cross-pollination with articles**: Petrarca's article knowledge system (claims, embeddings, knowledge index) could connect to book captures. Is this valuable, or does it dilute both experiences?

4. **What scheduling algorithm?** FSRS is overkill for open-ended prompts. Readwise's decay-based half-life is simpler. But neither was designed for "generative re-encounter" rather than "test recall."

5. **The chapter structure advantage**: Physical books have chapters. Articles typically don't. Can chapter-level structure enable better synthesis than article-level processing?
