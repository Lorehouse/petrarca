What the Research Says

Learning Science for Adult Knowledge Retention

Findings from cognitive psychology, spaced repetition research, curriculum design theory, and Petrarca's own calibration experiments — filtered for what's actionable in software for an adult reader outside formal school.

1. Flashcards Fail for Conceptual Knowledge

Traditional SRS (Anki, SuperMemo) reinforces single prompt-response links. This works for vocabulary and dates but breaks down for arguments, frameworks, and causal reasoning — exactly the knowledge books provide.

Scott H. Young SRS is "fantastic for discrete pieces of information" but "struggles with conceptual understanding" because it reinforces isolated links while conceptual understanding requires understanding how ideas relate to and depend on each other.
LessWrong Community Critique After using SRS "religiously for 3 years," users report insufficient returns for complex material. Card-creation burden is enormous for conceptual content, and cards tend to be either too simple (missing the concept) or too complex (effectively re-reading a paragraph).

What SRS gets right: The spacing effect itself is one of the most robust findings in cognitive psychology (10–30% retention improvement per meta-analysis of 254 studies). The problem isn't spacing — it's the flashcard format. The spacing effect works through any repeated encounter at expanding intervals, especially with active processing.

2. Elaborative Retrieval Dramatically Outperforms Recognition

Karpicke & Blunt, 2011 — Science Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping. Crucially, the advantage held even when the test required creating concept maps — the active ingredient is retrieval itself.

The research reveals a clear hierarchy of retrieval effectiveness:

FormatMechanismRetention Impact
Free recall "What do you remember about X?" Strongest — relational + item-specific processing
Connection prompts "How does X relate to Y?" Very strong — elaborative encoding
Explanation prompts "Why did the author argue X?" Strong — causal model building
Cued recall "What was the author's thesis about X?" Moderate — item-specific only
Card-flip recognition ← current "Did you know this? Knew / Partly / Missed" Weakest — familiarity only, not recollection

Implication: Petrarca's current review-session.tsx uses the weakest format. Moving to free recall (voice-based) or connection prompts would dramatically improve retention with the same time investment.

3. Natural Spacing Through Reading Beats Artificial Review

Reading a new article that touches concepts from a previously-read book creates "natural spacing" with several advantages over SRS:

  • Novel context — concept appears in genuinely new context, encoding additional retrieval cues
  • Incidental retrieval — reader's recognition of a familiar concept triggers retrieval of the original source
  • Elaborative connection — reader naturally compares and contrasts the two sources' perspectives
  • Intrinsic motivation — the new article is interesting in its own right, not a chore
Bjork & Bjork, Desirable Difficulties Framework Interleaved practice produced 43% better performance on delayed tests vs. blocked practice. A reading feed that naturally interleaves articles from different books' topics provides interleaving benefits automatically.

Petrarca already creates this — 769 claim→node links map articles to the same curriculum nodes as books. But the reader is never told "this article just reinforced concepts from your book." Making this visible transforms passive consumption into active knowledge maintenance.

4. Gist Memory Outlasts Detail Memory

Brainerd & Reyna — Fuzzy-Trace Theory People encode two parallel memory traces: verbatim (surface form, exact details) and gist (essential meaning, the argument). "Verbatim traces become inaccessible faster than gist traces." Gist memories persist months to years for well-understood material.

The retention timeline without reinforcement:

TimeWhat Remains
1 weekMain arguments, key examples, resonant passages
1 monthMain thesis clear, specific arguments fuzzy, examples gone
3 monthsCan state the book's "big idea" but not reconstruct its argument
1 yearVague sense of "having read something about X"

Key insight: The system should reinforce gist occasionally (to prevent even gist decay), and re-connect details to gist when encountered in new contexts. Testing for verbatim recall of names and dates is the wrong target.

5. Test-Potentiated Learning — Failed Attempts Are Valuable

Arnold & McDermott, 2013 Even unsuccessful retrieval attempts, followed by feedback, produce stronger learning than successful retrieval alone. This is "test-potentiated learning" — the struggle is the active ingredient.
Pastötter & Bäuml, 2014 The "forward effect of testing" — testing on material A improves subsequent learning of material B, even unrelated material. The act of retrieval primes the encoding machinery.

Implication: When a user answers "missed" in the review session, that's the most valuable learning moment. The system should respond with enriched context — temporal hooks, cross-book connections, vivid details — not the same brief answer guidance shown to someone who got it right.

6. Comparative Judgment Is More Reliable Than Absolute Self-Report

Thurstone's Law of Comparative Judgment Asking "Which do you know more about: X or Y?" produces more reliable data than "How well do you know X?" (1–5 scale). Each comparison gives two data points simultaneously. Adults calibrate significantly better on relative judgments than absolute ratings.

Petrarca's curriculum scan currently uses absolute 3-level assessment (new/some/all). Mixing in comparative pairs — "Which do you know more about: Athenian democracy or Spartan military society?" — would be faster, more reliable, and feel less like a test.

7. The Michel Thomas Principle

From Petrarca's own user research — perhaps the most important constraint of all:

User Feedback (Session 26) "The fear of being overwhelmed and the fear of forgetting has been stopping me from reading interesting nonfiction." Michel Thomas' language method tells students: "Don't try to remember. I'll manage your memory for you." That freedom from anxiety actually improves learning.

Every feature must pass this test: does it make reading feel lighter, or heavier? The knowledge map should feel like liberation ("look how much I know!"), not homework ("look how much I've forgotten").

Design Constraints

What Shapes the Solution Space

These aren't just limitations — they're design principles that make the system honest.

📖
Books are encoding; system is maintenance. The system never competes with books for the learning role. Books provide narrative, voice, argument, structure — the richest encoding. The system's job is connecting, resurfacing, and preventing decay. Never teach; always remind and connect.
🧠
Single adult user, not a classroom. No multi-user abstractions. The reader has deep prior knowledge, strong metacognition, and idiosyncratic interests. Self-report is more reliable than for novices (experts calibrate better). But also — this user has limited time and infinite curiosity. Zero-friction or nothing.
⏱
Stolen moments, not study sessions. Reading happens on buses, at lunch, in the evening. A chapter-complete card must be dismissable in 1 tap. A review session must be valuable in 3 minutes. If it feels like homework, it dies.
🗺
Multiple bounded curricula, not one world graph. Knowledge is modeled as overlapping college-course-sized domains (50–80 nodes each). Power comes from intersections — nexus entities that light up across curricula. Avoids the Otak failure mode (67K-node fractal graph that lost coherence).
🔄
Knowledge doesn't decay like flashcards. Gist persists for months/years. Details fade first. The system shouldn't model binary forgetting (FSRS). Instead: gradient confidence that fades naturally but resets through related reading encounters. "You probably still know the gist — let me reconnect a detail."
🖥
Server-first, LLM-generated. All state lives on the server. Reviews, questions, connections are generated via Gemini Flash (speed) or Claude Opus (quality). No pre-authored card decks. Everything is dynamic and contextual.
📊
Calibrated thresholds. Every similarity threshold has been human-calibrated: KNOWN ≥ 0.82, EXTENDS ≥ 0.74, curriculum mapping ≥ 0.65, article similarity AUROC = 0.930. New features should operate within this calibrated space, not introduce uncalibrated magic numbers.
Ten Ideas

Actionable Proposals

Each proposal maps a specific research finding to Petrarca's existing infrastructure. Ordered by expected impact × feasibility.

1

Chapter-Complete Cards with Temporal Hooks

Built Impact: Very High As: Manual chapter preview/review buttons

When you select a new chapter in the book detail dropdown, a brief card appears showing what you just covered and connecting it to what you already know. One tap dismisses it. Value accumulates over weeks of reading.

Example: Finishing Chapter 4 of "A History of Sicily"

Chapter 4: The Tyranny of Dionysius

✦ Dionysian tyranny — now engaged   ✦ Carthaginian wars — anchored

While Dionysius was fortifying Syracuse (405 BC), Plato was still a student of Socrates in Athens — he wouldn't visit Syracuse for another 17 years.

Quick check: Why did Syracuse need a tyrant at this moment? [new / some / knew]

Data needed: chapter→node mapping (built), temporal cross-references from node dates (enriched), knowledge state (tracked). Zero new algorithms — orchestration only.
Research Backing

Elaborative encoding through temporal association (generation effect): connecting a new fact to a known anchor makes it dramatically more durable. The Hamarquizen pattern ("Hanna Winsnes born 1789 = French Revolution") proved this works for both children and adults.

Micro-testing at encoding time: The quick self-assessment ("new / some / knew") exploits the testing effect — even asking the question, with no consequences for getting it wrong, improves later recall by 50% compared to just reading (Roediger & Karpicke 2006).

What Already Exists
  • map_chapter_to_nodes() — Maps chapters to 3–6 curriculum nodes via Gemini Flash
  • fill_prerequisite_gaps() — Creates items for prerequisites + time-period siblings
  • All 5 curricula enriched with date_start / date_end per node
  • Chapter complete endpoint: POST /review/chapter-complete exists on server
  • Design doc with full UX spec in reading-companion-process-design.md
What Needs Building
  • Temporal hook generation: For each node covered by the chapter, find nodes in other curricula with overlapping dates where the reader has knowledge ≥ "engaged." Select the most vivid connection.
  • Chapter-complete card component: Brief, dismissable card in book detail screen. Shows nodes, hook, and single self-assessment question.
  • Chapter tracking persistence: chapter_completions: Record<number, timestamp> field on book object.
Key Design Decision

Temporal hooks can be precomputed (faster, less contextual) or generated on-demand (richer, slower). Recommendation: precompute a pool of date-overlapping node pairs across curricula, then select from the pool based on the reader's current knowledge state. This gets the best of both: speed from precomputation, personalization from knowledge-aware selection.

2

"Reinforced From Your Reading" — Visible Natural Spacing

New Impact: High Effort: Small

After finishing an article, show a subtle note identifying which curriculum nodes from the reader's books were just reinforced. Makes the invisible knowledge maintenance visible.

After reading an article about Mediterranean naval warfare

✦ Reinforced from your reading

This article touched on Greek naval tactics and Syracusan alliances — topics you're covering in A History of Sicily. +2 nodes strengthened

Uses: article_curriculum_nodes from knowledge_index.json + book mappings. Computation is a simple set intersection — the data is already linked.
Research Backing

Natural spacing is the gold standard — encountering a concept in a new context (different article, different author, different angle) creates contextual variation, which encodes additional retrieval cues. This is better than flashcard review because the context is genuinely novel and the motivation is intrinsic.

Transfer-appropriate processing: Memory retrieval is best when cognitive processes at retrieval match those at encoding. Reading an article about the same topic = same deep-reading context as the original book. Much better match than a flashcard prompt.

The Michel Thomas effect: Making maintenance visible ("I'm tracking this for you") directly fulfills the "I'll manage your memory" promise. The reader feels cared for, not quizzed.

Implementation Detail

In the article reader's completion flow (after "Done" tap):

  1. Look up article_curriculum_nodes[articleId] from knowledge index
  2. For each node, check if any active book has a mapping to the same node
  3. If matches found, show reinforcement note with book title and node names
  4. Optionally update the curriculum knowledge state: bump confidence on matched nodes (source: "article:article_id")

Side effect: the feed ranking algorithm could use this data to boost articles that would reinforce active reading — articles touching curriculum nodes from in-progress books. The _getActiveBookTopicBoost function already exists but uses keyword matching; this would use the calibrated curriculum mapping instead.

Greek Colonization of Sicily

~ Partial: mentioned trade routes but not the specific connection to Magna Graecia

✗ Missing: the role of the Delphic oracle in sanctioning colonies, and the conflict with indigenous Sicels

Claim comparison uses amygdala's pairwise_cosine + classify_pairs against the curriculum node description + all book sources for that node. The VOICE_EXTRACT_PROMPT already exists in review_engine.py.
Research Backing

Free recall is the strongest retention format — promotes both relational and item-specific processing (research synthesis). Cued recall and recognition "do not appear to enhance conceptual organization and under some conditions can disrupt it."

The generation effect: actively producing information yourself produces better memory than passively receiving it. Speaking forces the user to reconstruct knowledge, identify gaps, and organize thoughts — all of which strengthen the memory network.

Self-explanation effect: explaining to yourself why something is true and how it connects is even more powerful than elaborative interrogation. Voice recall naturally triggers self-explanation as the user tries to articulate connections.

Existing Infrastructure
  • Soniox API integration (key in .env, patterns in alif + alignment)
  • VOICE_EXTRACT_PROMPT in review_engine.py — extracts remembered facts, questions, connections, suggested score
  • expo-av recording already used for book voice captures
  • amygdala pairwise_cosine for comparing extracted claims against node description
Design Decision: When to Use Each Mode

Don't replace card-flip entirely — offer both. Voice free recall is ideal for 5-minute dedicated review sessions (e.g., morning coffee). Card-flip suits quick checks during commutes when speaking isn't possible. The review session screen could offer a toggle: "Cards" vs. "Voice recall" at the top.

Pre-Reading Book Briefing

Built Impact: High As: "Preview this chapter" button in book detail

When starting a new book, show what you already know, what you'll learn, and what foundational knowledge you might be missing. Activates prior knowledge and sets expectations — two of the strongest encoding enhancers.

Pre-Reading Briefing for "The Normans in Sicily"

You already know well (6 nodes):

Byzantine administration of Sicily, Arab conquest, Emirate of Sicily, Greek colonial legacy, Syracuse as power center, Mediterranean trade networks

You'll likely learn about (8 nodes):

Norman conquest strategy, Roger I & II, Kingdom of Sicily, Norman-Arab-Byzantine synthesis, Palermo as cultural capital, Frederick II, papal conflicts, Sicilian Vespers

Shaky prerequisites (2 nodes):

Viking expansion context (mentioned, not engaged) — the Normans were originally Norse settlers in France. Investiture Controversy (unknown) — key context for papal politics that drives much of this period.

Computation: book→curriculum mapping (exists) × prerequisite graph (exists) × knowledge state (tracked). No new data — just a new view.
Research Backing

Prior knowledge activation: Schema theory shows that activating existing mental frameworks before new learning dramatically improves encoding. The mPFC "resonance detector" integrates new information directly when it fits pre-existing structures.

Zone of Proximal Development: Vygotsky's ZPD says you learn best at the frontier — where prerequisites are met but the concept is new. Flagging shaky prerequisites lets the reader choose: skip them (the book may cover it) or do a quick 2-minute review before starting.

Implementation

This is pure data orchestration over existing infrastructure:

  1. Run map_book_to_curriculum() to get the book's curriculum coverage (or use existing mapping)
  2. For each mapped node, classify by reader's knowledge state: known (anchored/engaged) vs. new (unknown/mentioned)
  3. For each new node, check if its prerequisites are in the reader's knowledge state. If not → "shaky prerequisite"
  4. Display as a section in the book detail screen, before/alongside BookCurriculumContext

Cross-Book Mini-Tutorials (PRIME→READ→TEST)

Built Impact: Very High As: Hamarquizen screen for finished books

On-demand 3–5 minute reviews that combine content from ALL books being read. Not a quiz — a rich mini-essay with temporal hooks, cross-book connections, and surprising juxtapositions. The Hamarquizen PRIME→READ→TEST pattern at scale.

Cross-Book Mini-Tutorial: "Syracuse Between Empires"

PRIME: Before we begin — what do you remember about why Syracuse kept switching allegiances between Greek and Carthaginian powers?

READ: In Norwich's A History of Sicily, you read about Dionysius playing Carthage against the Greek mainland. But in your Greek history reading, Athens' Sicilian Expedition (415 BC) shows the view from the other shore — Athens saw Sicily as the key to controlling the western Mediterranean. These are the same events, but Syracuse is the protagonist in one story and the target in the other.

What makes Syracuse extraordinary is its durability as a pivot point. Two centuries later, it played the same game with Rome and Carthage — and this time lost. Archimedes died in that final siege (212 BC), roughly 75 years after Alexander's death changed the entire Greek world.

TEST: Why did Syracuse's strategy of playing empires against each other ultimately fail with Rome, when it had worked for centuries with Carthage and Athens?

Generated via LLM (Opus for quality) using: book chapter claims + curriculum node descriptions + knowledge state + cross-book node overlaps. Personalized to what the reader actually knows.
Research Backing

Interleaving (Rohrer & Taylor 2007): 43% better delayed performance when mixing topics. Cross-book review inherently interleaves.

Elaborative retrieval: The comparison ("Norwich says X, your Greek history says Y") forces the reader to reconstruct and reconcile — the strongest retention mechanism.

Connection prompts are the most effective format: "How does X from Book A relate to Y from Book B?" This is exactly what cross-book tutorials generate.

Data Pipeline
  1. Identify curriculum nodes covered by 2+ active books (cross-book connections already computed)
  2. For each shared node, collect chapter claims from each book + the curriculum node description
  3. Include temporal hooks: nodes from other curricula with overlapping dates where reader has knowledge
  4. Generate PRIME→READ→TEST via Opus, with explicit instructions to compare perspectives and include at least one temporal hook
  5. Reader self-assesses at end → updates curriculum knowledge state
Key Design Decision

Must feel rich and surprising, not like a quiz. The "READ" section is a genuine mini-essay, not a list of facts. It should make the reader think "huh, I never connected those two things." This requires Opus-quality generation — Gemini Flash won't produce the right voice.

Knowledge-Aware Lens Selection

Easy Extension Impact: Medium Effort: Small

Currently, review lenses are selected based on review count (factual → ramp → analytical). Instead, select based on knowledge state — what kind of retrieval will strengthen this specific node most at its current level?

Knowledge StateBest LensWhy
Low confidence (<0.3) Recognition / Identity Establish the basics — "What was this?" (ZPD: below frontier = skip complex)
Medium (0.3–0.6) Causal / Significance Build framework — "Why did this matter?" (ZPD: at frontier = grow)
High (0.6–0.8) Comparative / Pattern Cross-reference — "How does this compare to X?" (strengthen gist)
Very high (>0.8) Consequence / Temporal Deepen framework — "What long-term effects?" (prevent gist decay)
Research Backing

Graduated depth assessment (ZPD): Testing below the frontier wastes time. Testing above it is frustrating. The sweet spot is 36–69% accuracy — where the reader knows enough to attempt but not enough to succeed easily.

Bloom's taxonomy applied to SRS (Jensen et al. 2014): Exams requiring higher-order thinking produce conceptual understanding that transfers to untested items. But only when lower levels are solid first.

Implementation

In review_engine.py's question generation, replace the review-count-based lens selection with confidence-based selection. The confidence value already exists on each knowledge_item. Map confidence ranges to lens types in the prompt.

Comparative Judgment in Curriculum Scans

New Impact: Medium Effort: Medium

Mix comparative pairs into the curriculum breadth scan. Every 3rd card, instead of asking about one node, show two nodes and ask "Which do you know more about?" This gives two data points per question and is psychometrically more reliable.

Comparative Assessment Card

Which do you know more about?

Athenian Democracy

Assembly, courts, citizenship reforms, Pericles' era

Spartan Military Society

Agoge training, dual kingship, helot system, Thermopylae

Tap the one you're more confident about  ·  "Neither" skips both

Bayesian update: selecting A over B constrains both simultaneously. Amygdala's EIG selection can pick the most informative pairs (highest joint entropy reduction).
Research Backing

Thurstone's law of comparative judgment: Two-item comparison is more reliable than absolute rating. ACJ (Adaptive Comparative Judgment) gives two data points per question.

Dunning-Kruger mitigation: Adults are bad at absolute self-assessment, especially for topics they know little about (they can't judge what they don't know). But comparative judgment bypasses this — you can judge relative familiarity even when both topics are vague.

Integration with Amygdala

Amygdala's knowledge_map module already implements entropy-maximizing probe selection. Extending it to support comparative (paired) questions alongside absolute questions would make the breadth scan converge faster — potentially reaching useful accuracy in 15 questions instead of 25.

Rich Failure Feedback — Lean Into "Missed"

Easy Extension Impact: High Effort: Small

When the user answers "missed" in a review session, show an expanded rich response — temporal hooks, cross-book connections, vivid context. Make failure the moment of richest learning, not a brief "here's what you should have known."

Current "Missed" Response

Dionysius I of Syracuse

Tyrant who seized power after Carthaginian destruction of Akragas. Built Syracuse into the strongest Greek city in the western Mediterranean.

Proposed "Missed" Response

Dionysius I of Syracuse

Seized power in 405 BC after Carthage destroyed Akragas — the crisis created demand for a strongman. Built the longest walls in the Greek world, invented the catapult (first recorded use), and made Syracuse the dominant western Greek power for 38 years.

Timeline anchor: Dionysius came to power 8 years after Athens' catastrophic Sicilian Expedition (413 BC) — the power vacuum that expedition created in Sicily made his rise possible.

📖 Covered in: A History of Sicily, Ch 4

Research Backing

Test-potentiated learning (Arnold & McDermott 2013): Failed retrieval + feedback produces stronger learning than successful retrieval. The struggle is the active ingredient — but the feedback quality determines how much is learned from the failure.

Alif pattern: When a learner forgets, respond with enrichment (more context, mnemonics, connections), not punishment. This is the Michel Thomas principle in action — "I'll manage your memory" means adding more hooks when something slips, not asking the user to try harder.

Implementation

Modify the question generation prompt (QUESTION_GEN_PROMPT) to always include a rich_answer field alongside answer_guidance. The rich answer includes: vivid detail, temporal hook, book source reference. In the review session UI, show answer_guidance for "knew" and rich_answer for "missed" and "partly."

Confidence Decay Model (Replace FSRS)

New Model Impact: Medium Effort: Medium

Replace the FSRS stability model with gradient confidence decay on curriculum nodes. Knowledge doesn't vanish like a flashcard — gist persists, details fade. The model should reflect this.

# Current: FSRS-style binary stability stability_days = old_stability * 2.5 # if knew due_at = now + stability_days * 86400000 # Proposed: Gradient confidence with engagement-depth half-lives HALF_LIVES = { 'surface_reading': 30, # days — detail fades fast 'chapter_complete': 60, # basic gist 'review_correct': 90, # tested gist 'elaborative_recall': 180, # voice free recall 'cross_book_synthesis': 365, # multiple sources 'article_reinforced': +30, # bonus: natural spacing } # Confidence decays as sigmoid def current_confidence(base_confidence, days_since, half_life): return base_confidence * 2 ** (-days_since / half_life)
Research Backing

Fuzzy-Trace Theory: Gist and verbatim traces decay at different rates. A system that models both (gist confidence with long half-life, detail confidence with short half-life) more accurately reflects reality than FSRS's single stability number.

Natural resurfacing resets decay: When an article touches a curriculum node from your book, that's a genuine re-encounter that should reset/extend the confidence half-life. FSRS has no mechanism for this — only explicit review resets stability.

Key Insight: Article Reading as Maintenance

The article_reinforced half-life bonus means that reading articles in the feed automatically maintains book knowledge — without the reader doing any explicit review. The system tracks which curriculum nodes were touched by article reading (via the 769 claim→node links) and adds a confidence bonus. This makes the feed itself a knowledge maintenance engine.

Forward Testing Warm-Ups Before New Chapters

New Impact: Medium Effort: Small

Before starting a new chapter, offer a 90-second warm-up: 3 quick questions reviewing the previous chapter's key nodes. Not to test retention — to prime the encoding machinery for the new material.

Warm-Up Before Starting Chapter 5

Quick warm-up from Chapter 4:

1. Why did Syracuse need a tyrant after 405 BC? [tap to check]

2. What military innovation did Dionysius introduce? [tap to check]

3. How did Dionysius relate to Plato's political philosophy? [tap to check]

Ready for Chapter 5: The Greek Golden Age in Sicily →

Questions drawn from knowledge_items mapped to previous chapter. Takes 60-90 seconds. The forward testing effect means even thinking about the answer (without checking) improves encoding of the next chapter.
Research Backing

Forward effect of testing (Pastötter & Bäuml 2014): Testing on material A improves subsequent learning of material B. The act of retrieval activates encoding mechanisms that persist into the next learning episode.

Prior knowledge activation: The warm-up also activates the specific knowledge network that the next chapter will build on — the reader starts Chapter 5 with Dionysius and Plato fresh in mind, making any references to them in the new chapter more salient.

Integration

This naturally combines with the chapter-complete card (Idea 1). The flow becomes: complete chapter → chapter card with temporal hook → start new chapter → 3 warm-up questions from previous chapter → read. The chapter card and warm-up can even be the same screen with two sections.

Bug Fix: Curriculum Mapping Was Blind to Chapter Content

map_book_to_curriculum() had two compounding bugs:

  1. Wrong file path — Looking for research at /opt/petrarca/data/books/research_{id}.json when files actually live at scripts/data/book_research/{id}.json. Research data was silently never found. The thesis and key_terms fields were always empty strings.
  2. Chapter research discarded — Even if the path were correct, only thesis and key_terms were extracted. The per-chapter data (summaries, claims, key terms) from chapter_research was ignored. The LLM received only chapter titles — "Ch 1: The Founding of Syracuse" — with no content to work with.

Fix: New helper functions (_load_book(), _load_book_research(), _format_chapters_with_research()) that read from the correct path, load from SQLite first, and format each chapter with its summary, claims, and key terms. The mapping prompt now receives:

Ch 1: The Founding of Syracuse Summary: This chapter chronicles the origins of Syracuse, tracing its development from prehistoric settlement... Key claims: Gelon established Syracuse as preeminent power by 485 BC; The ruling family held hereditary religious authority Terms: Ortygia, Gelon, Deinomenes Ch 2: Archaic Syracuse Summary: The emergence of Syracuse as a naval power... ... instead of just "Ch 1: The Founding of Syracuse, Ch 2: Archaic Syracuse"

Impact: Every future curriculum mapping will be dramatically more accurate. Existing mappings should be re-run for books with chapter research (Syracuse, Midnight in Sicily, Invention of Sicily have 8–15 chapters of research data).

Voice Elicitation — Free Recall as Review

A new review mode that replaces card-flip recognition with the strongest retrieval format from the research: free recall. Accessible from the ✦ drawer as "Voice Recall."

Candidate selection uses a scoring function that peaks at confidence = 0.5 and gives a bonus to "engaged" nodes — the ZPD frontier where free recall is most informative. Avoids "unknown" nodes (nothing to recall) and "anchored" nodes (already well-known).

The feedback card shows:

  • ✓ Captured — facts matched against node description + book sources (generous: paraphrases count)
  • ✗ Missed — the 2–3 most important omissions from the definition
  • ✦ Interesting — things the user said that go beyond the sources — connections, hypotheses, links
  • → Research queued — "I wonder..." statements extracted and added to the exploration queue
  • Coverage bar (percentage) + temporal hook + suggested score

The "wonderings" pipeline is the most novel part. If you say "I wonder whether Plato's visits to Syracuse influenced Roman political thought," the system detects it, creates a voice_followup review item due in 4 hours, and can eventually feed it into the topic research pipeline. Voice review becomes a knowledge generation mechanism, not just a testing mechanism.

Manual Chapter Preview / Review

Two buttons in the book detail screen, below the chapter dropdown: "Preview this chapter" and "Review this chapter". Never auto-triggered — always manual.

Preview Mode

What you'll learn: Nodes this chapter covers that are new to you

Shaky prerequisites: Prerequisites where your knowledge is low — flagged in rubric

Already known: Nodes you're strong on (anchored/engaged)

Activates prior knowledge before reading. Schema theory says this dramatically improves encoding.

Review Mode

What you covered: Curriculum nodes mapped from the chapter

Temporal hooks: Cross-curriculum connections for dated nodes

Quick self-assessment: One question with 3-tap answer (new/some/knew)

Consolidates encoding. The testing effect means even the question improves later recall by 50%.

Design decision: pull, not push. The original design called for auto-triggering on chapter change. We made it manual because: (a) you're often not at the book detail screen when you finish a chapter, (b) data quality varies — books without chapter research get weaker mappings, (c) the Michel Thomas principle says the system should feel helpful, not nagging. You tap when you want it.

Data flow: The endpoint calls map_chapter_to_nodes() (LLM-based, uses the now-fixed chapter research data), cross-references with the reader's knowledge state, and computes prerequisite gaps. The modal shows knowledge dots (green/teal/orange/gray) for each node.

Hamarquizen — PRIME→READ→TEST for Finished Books

A new review format for books you've already read. Generates vivid micro-narratives with immediate quizzes. Answers enter the FSRS review pool for long-term retention.

Example Card: Cicero's Roman Republic

PRIME: What do you remember about Cicero's role in the Catiline conspiracy?

READ: In 63 BC, Cicero as consul uncovered Catiline's plot to overthrow the Republic. His four orations against Catiline — delivered in the Senate — became the most famous political speeches in Roman history. But his decision to execute the conspirators without trial haunted him: it gave Clodius the legal weapon to exile him three years later.

TEST: Why did executing the Catilinarian conspirators ultimately weaken Cicero's position?

ANSWER: Executing citizens without trial was technically illegal, giving political enemies (especially Clodius) grounds to prosecute him for overstepping consular authority.

✦ Temporal hook: Cicero's consulship (63 BC) was just 17 years before Caesar crossed the Rubicon

The READ section has a distinctive rubric left-border in the app. Each scored answer updates FSRS: knew ×2.5 stability, partly ×1.5, missed ×0.4.

Entry points:

  • Book detail: "✦ Hamarquizen Review" button appears when reading_status is finished or read
  • ✦ Drawer: "Hamarquizen" entry (requires book selection)

Card generation: Queries knowledge_items for the book, orders by lowest confidence first (review what's fading), generates PRIME→READ→TEST via Gemini Flash with the HAMARQUIZEN_PROMPT. The prompt demands concrete names, dates, and one surprising temporal anchor per card.

Key difference from regular review: The READ section. Regular review cards show question → answer. Hamarquizen shows question → vivid narrative → quiz → answer. The narrative re-encodes the knowledge with rich context, not just tests whether it's there. This is the "test-potentiated learning" mechanism from the research — even failed attempts followed by rich feedback produce stronger learning.

Bigger Open Questions

📚
How much can we know about books we don't have the text of? Chapter research is Gemini-inferred from titles + web search. For well-known books (Norwich, Iggulden) this is decent. For obscure books, it's guesswork. The real quality leap would come from: (a) photographing key pages, (b) Kindle highlights, (c) voice captures summarizing chapters. The system should actively invite these when chapter data is thin.
🧩
Should the concrete knowledge SRS and the conceptual understanding system be separate? The user's insight is right: dates, places, and wars ARE the scaffolding — without them, conceptual understanding collapses. But the research shows they need different retrieval formats. The current system uses the same card-flip for both. A possible split: factual drill (dates, names → standard SRS cards, high repetition) + elaborative review (significance, connections → Hamarquizen/voice, lower frequency). The curriculum node's bloom_floor field (recognize/explain/analyze) could drive this split.
🔄
How should article reading feed back into the book knowledge system? Currently, articles map to curriculum nodes (769 links) and books map to curriculum nodes — but the connection is invisible. When you read an article about Greek naval warfare, it's reinforcing the same curriculum nodes as your Sicily book. The system should: (a) tell you this happened (#2), (b) bump confidence on those nodes, and (c) use this as a "natural spacing" event that extends the review interval. This turns the feed into a passive knowledge maintenance engine.
🎯
What's the right depth for curricula? Current curricula have 50–70 nodes each. Is this enough? The AP World History framework has ~150–300 assessable nodes per domain. But these are "educated adult" curricula, not course-prep. The answer may be: start at 50–70 and deepen organically — when the user shows sustained interest in a subtopic (via reviews, voice recalls, article reading), auto-suggest expanding that branch.
