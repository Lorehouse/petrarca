## 1. Major Historical Thinking Frameworks

Four major national frameworks have shaped how historical thinking is taught and assessed globally. They share significant overlap but emphasize different dimensions.

### 1.1 Seixas' "Big Six" Historical Thinking Concepts (Canada)

**Peter Seixas** (University of British Columbia) led The Historical Thinking Project, producing the most widely adopted framework. Published with Tom Morton in *The Big Six Historical Thinking Concepts* (Nelson Education, 2013).

The six concepts, each with specific "guideposts" (progression indicators):

1. **Historical Significance** — How do we decide what is important to learn about the past?
   - Events gain significance when they are part of a larger narrative relevant to present concerns
   - Criteria: resulted in change (profundity — how deeply), affected many people (quantity), lasted a long time (durability), relevant to understanding the present
   - Significance is not inherent — it is *established* by historians making choices

2. **Primary Source Evidence** — How do we know what we know about the past?
   - History is interpretation based on inferences from primary sources
   - Good questions turn a source into evidence
   - Inferences must always be corroborated against other sources
   - Context shapes interpretation

3. **Continuity and Change** — How can we make sense of the complex flows of history?
   - Must identify both what changed AND what stayed the same
   - Change is not always progress
   - Periodization is a tool for organizing change/continuity

4. **Cause and Consequence** — Why do events happen, and what are their impacts?
   - Long-term ideologies, institutions, and conditions vs. short-term motivations, actions, events
   - Multiple causes interact; consequences are not always intended
   - Agency: humans cause change that impacts present-day issues

5. **Historical Perspectives** — How can we better understand the people of the past?
   - See through the eyes of people in times far removed from our present
   - Avoid presentism — don't judge past people by present-day values
   - Requires contextual knowledge to understand worldviews

6. **The Ethical Dimension** — How can history help us live in the present?
   - Learn about moral issues today by examining the past
   - Reserve present-day moral judgments to understand historical actions without approving of them

**Assessment approach:** Each concept has guideposts with beginner/intermediate/proficient descriptors. The framework was designed for classroom-based formative assessment, not standardized testing.

**Key resource:** Seixas, P. (2006). "Benchmarks of Historical Thinking: A Framework for Assessment in Canada." *Canadian Journal of Education*.

### 1.2 Wineburg's Historical Thinking Skills (USA — Stanford/SHEG)

**Sam Wineburg** (Stanford) founded the Stanford History Education Group (now Digital Inquiry Group). His work, grounded in cognitive science think-aloud studies, identifies four core cognitive strategies that historians use but students must learn:

1. **Sourcing** — Before reading a document's content, identify who wrote it, when, and why. Evaluate trustworthiness based on the source's position and purpose.

2. **Contextualization** — Situate the document within the broader temporal, spatial, and social context. What was happening when this was written?

3. **Corroboration** — Compare multiple documents about the same event. Where do accounts agree? Where do they diverge? Why might they differ?

4. **Close Reading** — Read carefully for the author's specific claims, the evidence marshaled, and the reasoning used. What is actually being said vs. implied?

**Assessment tools produced:**
- **"Reading Like a Historian" (RLH)** curriculum — Document-based lessons using primary sources. 15+ million downloads. Quasi-experimental studies showed significant gains on all four dimensions: historical thinking, transfer to contemporary issues, factual knowledge, and reading comprehension.
- **"Beyond the Bubble" (HATs)** — 150+ History Assessments of Thinking, each taking only minutes. Short formative tasks aligned to Library of Congress primary sources. Three-level rubrics: basic, emergent, proficient. Free via beyondthebubble.stanford.edu. Assess sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, periodization, and argumentation.

**Key insight for Petrarca:** Wineburg showed that historical thinking is "unnatural" — it requires deliberately overriding our natural tendency to read the past through present-day lenses. This aligns with Petrarca's design principle #5 (Historical Perspectives).

### 1.3 UCLA/NCHS Five Historical Thinking Standards (USA — National)

The **National Center for History in the Schools** at UCLA developed five interconnected standards, funded federally as part of national education reform:

1. **Chronological Thinking** — "At the heart of historical reasoning." Includes:
   - Distinguish past/present/future
   - Identify temporal structure of narratives (beginning, middle, end)
   - Construct narratives forward from origins or backward from outcomes
   - Calculate calendar time (decades, centuries, millennia; BC/BCE and AD/CE)
   - Interpret and create timelines with appropriate intervals
   - Reconstruct patterns of succession and duration; explain continuity/change
   - Compare alternative periodization models

2. **Historical Comprehension** — Reconstruct literal meaning, identify central questions, draw on visual/literary/quantitative data, read historical narratives imaginatively.

3. **Historical Analysis and Interpretation** — Compare competing narratives, consider multiple perspectives, hold interpretive hypotheses as tentative, evaluate major debates among historians.

4. **Historical Research Capabilities** — Formulate questions, obtain and interrogate data from varied sources, marshal evidence for arguments.

5. **Historical Issues-Analysis and Decision-Making** — Identify problems addressed by past decision-makers, analyze interests/values/motives at play, evaluate consequences.

These standards are cumulative and "interactive and mutually supportive" — they cannot be developed in isolation.

### 1.4 Van Drie & Van Boxtel's Historical Reasoning Framework (Netherlands)

**Jannet van Drie and Carla van Boxtel** (University of Amsterdam) produced a framework emphasizing historical reasoning as an "integrative and socially situated activity." Their model identifies six components:

1. **Asking historical questions**
2. **Using sources**
3. **Contextualization** — Situating phenomena in temporal, spatial, and social context
4. **Argumentation**
5. **Using substantive concepts** (e.g., feudalism, revolution)
6. **Using meta-concepts** (e.g., cause, change, evidence)

They distinguish three *types* of historical reasoning: explaining, comparing, and evaluating change. Importantly, they found that persons with more domain expertise are more inclined to ask questions — questioning itself is a marker of understanding.

### 1.5 UK Schools History Project and Lee/Ashby Progression Model (England)

The **Schools Council History Project** (1970s) embedded disciplinary methods of historical enquiry into teaching. This led to the influential distinction between:

- **First-order (substantive) concepts** — Names, dates, events, people, places (the content)
- **Second-order (procedural/disciplinary) concepts** — Cause, evidence, change, significance, perspective (the meta-thinking)

**Peter Lee and Rosalyn Ashby** (Institute of Education, London) studied progression through the CHATA project (Concepts of History and Teaching Approaches, 7-14). They tracked 320 students aged 7-14 responding to task-sets about evidence and causation. Their key finding: students progress through qualitatively different stages of understanding about what evidence *is* and how causes work, not just accumulating more factual knowledge.

The UK Ofsted Research Review (2021) emphasizes that teachers should avoid mark schemes that reward "giving a judgement" without assessing the *historical quality* of that judgement — a warning against shallow rubrics.

## 2. AP History Exam Design and Rubrics

The College Board's AP History exams (AP European History, AP World History: Modern, AP US History) represent the most widely deployed standardized assessment of historical thinking in the English-speaking world.

### 2.1 Exam Structure

| Component | Time | Weight | Description |
|-----------|------|--------|-------------|
| Multiple Choice | 55 min | 40% | 55 questions, stimulus-based (primary/secondary sources) |
| Short Answer Questions | 40 min | 20% | 3-4 questions requiring brief written analysis |
| Document-Based Question (DBQ) | 60 min (incl. 15 min reading) | 25% | 7 documents, multiple perspectives on a historical development |
| Long Essay Question (LEQ) | 40 min | 15% | Choose 1 of 3 options spanning different time periods |

### 2.2 The DBQ Rubric (7 points)

This is the most sophisticated rubric for assessing historical thinking at scale:

**A. Thesis/Claim (1 point)**
- Must be historically defensible
- Must establish a line of reasoning (not merely restate the prompt)
- Located in introduction or conclusion

**B. Contextualization (1 point)**
- Describe broader historical events/developments/processes that occur before, during, or after the prompt's timeframe
- Must be more than merely a phrase or reference — requires meaningful description

**C. Evidence (2 points)**
- Point 1: Accurately describe (not merely quote) content from at least 3 documents
- Point 2: Support the argument using at least 6 documents, explaining how document content supports the claim

**D. Analysis and Reasoning — Sourcing (1 point)**
- For at least 3 documents, explain how or why the document's point of view, purpose, historical situation, or audience is relevant to the argument
- This is the "HIPP" analysis: Historical context, Intended audience, Purpose, Point of view

**E. Analysis and Reasoning — Outside Evidence (1 point)**
- Use at least one piece of evidence not found in the documents to support the argument

**F. Complexity (1 point)** — The hardest point to earn
Demonstrate complex understanding by doing one or more of:
- Explaining nuance by analyzing multiple variables
- Explaining both similarity AND difference, or continuity AND change, or multiple causes, or cause AND effect
- Making relevant connections within and across periods
- Corroborating multiple perspectives across themes
- Qualifying or modifying an argument by considering diverse/alternative views

Must be part of the argument, not merely a phrase.

### 2.3 The LEQ Rubric (6 points)

Similar structure: Thesis (1), Contextualization (1), Evidence (2), Analysis/Reasoning (1), Complexity (1). The evidence points focus on specific examples rather than documents.

### 2.4 Key Insight: What "Complexity" Means

The AP complexity point is the most relevant rubric element for Petrarca's purposes. It captures what distinguishes genuine historical understanding from competent recall:
- Not just knowing what happened, but understanding *why* it was complicated
- Seeing multiple causation, unintended consequences, competing perspectives
- Making connections across time periods and themes
- Qualifying claims rather than making absolute statements

**Implication for Petrarca:** A user who says "The Roman Republic fell because of military expansion" is at the evidence level. A user who says "Military expansion created conditions for both economic inequality and the rise of powerful generals, but these interacted with existing tensions in the Senate — and the Republic's institutions may have been more resilient than we think, since the transition took over a century" is demonstrating complexity.

## 3. Standardized Instruments and Tests

### 3.1 HiTCH Test (Historical Thinking — Competencies in History)

The largest validated standardized instrument for historical thinking, developed by a consortium of 9 universities across Germany, Switzerland, and Austria. Based on the FUER competency model.

- **91 closed-ended (multiple-choice) items** continuously standardized
- Validated in pilot tests with **2,500 students** in a large-scale procedure
- Tested in two large tablet-based studies
- Assesses four competencies: formulating historical questions, methodological competence (source analysis), narrative competence, and temporal orientation
- Under constant development; facets are not yet equally represented

**Key limitation:** Closed-task format means it primarily assesses recognition rather than production of historical thinking. The developers acknowledge this trade-off between scalability and depth.

### 3.2 NAEP U.S. History Assessment

The National Assessment of Educational Progress ("The Nation's Report Card") assesses U.S. history at grades 4, 8, and 12 with three achievement levels:

**Grade 12 levels (most relevant for Petrarca's adult user):**
- **Basic (294):** Identify significance of major historical figures, events, and documents; recognize America's diversity and changing global relationships
- **Proficient (325):** Understand historical context with awareness of multiple influencing factors; communicate reasoned interpretations using historical evidence effectively
- **Advanced (355):** Critically evaluate historical claims; understand how interpretations change over time; compose well-reasoned arguments drawing from extensive sources

The NAEP framework defines historical understanding as a "dynamic interplay between content knowledge and intellectual (cognitive) skills." It organizes content around four themes: Democracy, Cultures, Technology, and World Role.

### 3.3 Beliefs about History Questionnaire (BHQ)

Developed by **Liliana Maggioni** in collaboration with VanSledright and Alexander. A 22-item instrument assessing students' epistemological beliefs about history:
- 5 items: Copier/Objectivist scale
- 9 items: Borrower/Subjectivist scale
- 8 items: Criterialist scale

(See Section 4 for the three stances.)

Validated but with known difficulties: categorizing respondents within a single stage often proves challenging, and pre-/post-test changes can appear random. The instrument is better at identifying tendencies than placing people in discrete stages.

### 3.4 Beyond the Bubble HATs (Stanford/DIG)

Over 150 short formative assessments using Library of Congress documents. Three-level rubrics (basic, emergent, proficient) for each HAT. Each assessment has annotated examples of student work at each rubric level. Free and Creative Commons licensed.

HATs assess: sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, periodization, argumentation, close reading, use of evidence, and historical significance.

**Most applicable to Petrarca:** These are designed to be *quick* (a few minutes each) and *formative* (informing next steps rather than grading). This aligns with Petrarca's need for lightweight, ongoing assessment embedded in the reading experience.

## 4. Epistemic Cognition and Progression Models

### 4.1 Maggioni-VanSledright Three Epistemic Stances

Building on developmental epistemology research, Maggioni, VanSledright, and Alexander identified three stances that characterize how people think about what historical knowledge *is*:

**Copier/Objectivist Stance:**
- History is a fixed, singular copy of the past
- Sources yield direct, unproblematic information
- The only question is whether information is correct or incorrect
- "History is what happened"

**Subjectivist/Borrower Stance:**
- History is mainly a matter of unconstrained accounts by different historians
- Since everyone has opinions, all interpretations are equally valid
- There's no reliable way to judge between competing accounts
- "History is just opinions"

**Criterialist Stance:**
- History is constructed by authors following certain procedural principles
- Interpretations can be evaluated using disciplinary criteria (evidence, argumentation, perspective awareness)
- Some accounts are better supported than others, even if none are final
- "History is evidence-based interpretation"

**The progression is not linear.** Students may exhibit different stances depending on the topic or context. The jump from Subjectivist to Criterialist is particularly difficult and requires explicit instruction.

**Implication for Petrarca:** The system should assess not just what the user knows factually, but what epistemic stance they're operating from. A user who reads competing accounts of the fall of Rome and says "nobody really knows" is at the Subjectivist stage. A user who says "Ward-Perkins makes a stronger case than Goldsworthy because he uses archaeological evidence of economic decline" is operating at the Criterialist level.

### 4.2 Lee-Shemilt Progression Model (Evidence)

Through the CHATA project, Lee and Shemilt identified stages of how students understand historical evidence:

1. **Pictures of the past** — Sources are direct windows onto what happened
2. **Information** — Sources contain information that may be right or wrong
3. **Testimony** — Sources are reports from witnesses; reliability matters
4. **Scissors and paste** — Historians cut out reliable bits and assemble them
5. **Evidence in isolation** — Sources can be interrogated in context for implicit meaning
6. **Evidence in context** — Sources are understood as embedded in webs of other evidence; interpretation is always provisional

Students who see the past and history as analogous concepts tend to treat evidence as direct access. Students who can conceive of history as a *construct* have more nuanced views of interpretation.

### 4.3 Rüsen's Four Types of Historical Consciousness

**Jörn Rüsen** (Germany) theorized four developmental types of historical consciousness, each with different implications for how people use the past:

1. **Traditional** — The past as a model for unchanging values ("This is how we've always done it")
2. **Exemplary** — The past as a source of lessons and rules ("History repeats itself")
3. **Critical** — The past as something to be questioned and deconstructed ("Whose story is being told?")
4. **Genetic** — The past as a process of development and transformation ("How did we get here?")

Rüsen's core concept is **"narrative competence"** — the ability to construct meaningful narratives that connect past, present, and future. This is assessed through the quality and structure of the historical narratives a person can produce.

**Key link to Petrarca:** Rüsen's genetic type maps closely onto Petrarca's design principle about temporal hooks and causal chains. A user developing genetic historical consciousness can place events in developmental sequences — exactly what the curriculum node structure aims to scaffold.

### 4.4 Shemilt's "Big Picture" Frameworks

**Denis Shemilt** proposed that history education should build synoptic frameworks — "big pictures" of the past — that anchor all other learning:

**Four levels of narrative frameworks:**
1. **Chronologically ordered past** — History as a map; timelines with varying detail
2. **Coherent narratives** — History as a story; every event has meaning within the narrative
3. **Multidimensional narratives** — Three interlocking dimensions: (a) means of production/population, (b) social organization, (c) cultural/intellectual history
4. **Analytical frameworks** — Multiple analytical lenses applied simultaneously

Shemilt's (2009) research with 11-17-year-olds showed that structured frameworks can accelerate students' formation of "usable bigger pictures" of the past. Having such a big picture is necessary for developing historical consciousness.

**Direct parallel to Petrarca:** This is essentially what Petrarca's curriculum nodes aim to be — a structured framework that helps the user build and fill in a "big picture." The progression from level 1 (timeline) to level 3 (multidimensional) maps onto the progression from `unknown` through `mentioned` and `engaged` to `anchored`.

## 5. Historical Significance Criteria

Multiple scholars have proposed frameworks for determining what makes historical events or people significant — directly applicable to how Petrarca decides what knowledge to surface and prioritize.

### 5.1 Partington's Five Factors (1980)
1. **Importance** — To people living at the time
2. **Profundity** — How deeply lives were affected
3. **Quantity** — How many lives were affected
4. **Durability** — For how long lives were affected
5. **Relevance** — How much it contributes to understanding present life

### 5.2 Counsell's Five Rs (2004)
1. **Remarkable** — Commented on by people at the time and since
2. **Remembered** — A turning point in history, widely known
3. **Resulted in change** — Led to demonstrable consequences
4. **Resonant** — Allows meaningful analogies across time
5. **Revealing** — Illuminates other aspects of the past or present

### 5.3 Cercadillo's Six Types (2006)
1. **Contemporary significance** — Important at the time
2. **Causal significance** — Important because of what it caused
3. **Pattern significance** — Illustrates a broader pattern
4. **Symbolic significance** — Has come to represent something larger
5. **Revelatory significance** — Reveals something otherwise hidden
6. **Present significance** — Important for understanding today

**Implication for Petrarca:** When the system generates review questions or determines what to highlight, these significance criteria provide principled ways to prioritize. A claim that has causal significance AND resonance AND present significance is more important to reinforce than one that only has contemporary significance. This could inform the scoring algorithm for review items.

## 6. Expert Knowledge Organization in History

### 6.1 How Historians Organize Knowledge

Research shows historians organize knowledge along multiple simultaneous dimensions:

- **Period** (medieval, early modern, etc.) — but periodization is itself a contested analytical choice
- **Geography** (Mediterranean, East Asian, etc.)
- **Theme** (economic, political, social, cultural, intellectual, military, environmental)
- **Approach** (comparative, transnational, subaltern, gender, etc.)

Most historians specialize by combining period + geography + theme (e.g., "early modern Mediterranean economic history"). This creates a deeply interconnected web within a domain, with sparse but meaningful connections to other domains.

The chronic history-teaching debate is "chronological vs. thematic" organization. Research suggests neither alone is sufficient — the most effective frameworks use chronological structure enriched with thematic analysis, or thematic organization with strong chronological anchoring.

### 6.2 The Thematic-Chronological Integration

Shemilt's "multidimensional narrative" framework proposes three interlocking analytical tracks that run in parallel across time:
1. Modes of production and population history
2. Forms of social/political organization
3. Cultural and intellectual developments

This maps remarkably well onto Petrarca's curriculum structure, where each domain has multiple cross-cutting nodes that can be traversed both chronologically and thematically.

### 6.3 Expert vs. Novice Reading of History

Wineburg's think-aloud studies revealed that expert historians, when reading a new primary source:
1. **Source first** — Look at attribution before reading content
2. **Contextualize immediately** — Place the source in its historical moment
3. **Read with suspicion** — Ask "why is this person saying this?"
4. **Construct a subtext** — What is not being said?
5. **Monitor comprehension** — Notice confusion and formulate questions

Novices read primary sources like they read textbooks — as neutral conveyors of information, evaluating only whether the content seems "right" or "wrong."

## 7. Computational Approaches to Historical Knowledge

### 7.1 Causal Micro-Narratives (NLP Research)

Recent work on **Causal Micro-Narratives** (2024, arxiv:2410.05252) classifies sentence-level causal explanations from text — cause(s) and/or effect(s) of a target subject. Using human-annotated data from historical and contemporary US news articles:
- Fine-tuned Llama 3.1 8B achieves **F1 = 0.87 on narrative detection** and **F1 = 0.71 on classification**
- This suggests automated detection of causal reasoning in text is feasible

### 7.2 HistoryBankQA (Microsoft Research, 2025)

A multilingual benchmark for temporal question answering on historical events:
- Based on **HistoryBank** — 10M+ historical events extracted from Wikipedia timeline pages and article infoboxes across 10 languages
- Six temporal QA reasoning tasks:
  1. **FactQA** — Explicit date retrieval
  2. **SequenceQA** — Event sequencing and ordering
  3. **DurationQA** — Temporal interval computation
  4. **RelationQA** — Relative time expression resolution
  5. **CountQA** — Counting events in a period
  6. **RecurrenceQA** — Identifying previous/next occurrences

GPT-4o performs best; smaller models struggle especially with sequencing and duration tasks. This suggests temporal reasoning is a meaningful and measurable dimension of historical knowledge.

### 7.3 LLM-Based Essay Scoring

Systematic reviews (2024-2025) of 49+ studies on LLM-powered assessment show:
- Fine-tuned GPT-3.5 models show superior accuracy and consistency for essay scoring
- GPT-4 demonstrates excellent intrarater reliability
- LLMs handle grammar/structure well but show **significant discrepancy for content-related criteria** — they lack the deep semantic understanding humans have
- LLMs are better at identifying structural features of argumentation than evaluating the quality of historical claims

**Implication for Petrarca:** LLMs can reliably assess *structural* features of historical reasoning (presence of thesis, use of evidence, contextual framing) but not yet the *substantive* accuracy of historical claims. The system should use LLMs to assess reasoning quality while relying on its knowledge base for factual accuracy.

### 7.4 Concept Mapping and Causal Network Analysis

Collaborative concept mapping research shows students who construct cause-and-effect maps engage in more frequent causality construction and draw on prior knowledge more often. Computationally, causal relationships between historical events can be represented as directed graphs, with connection strength measurable through participant judgments or automated extraction.

## 8. Digital History Tools and Knowledge Bases

### 8.1 Event-Centric Knowledge Graphs

**EventKG** (L3S Research Center, Hannover) is the most comprehensive event-centric temporal knowledge graph:
- 1.3M+ events, 4.5M+ temporal relations
- Draws from Wikidata, DBpedia, YAGO, and Wikipedia event lists in 15 languages
- RDF-modeled, accessible via public SPARQL endpoint
- Can generate biographical and event timelines
- About half of events come from existing knowledge graphs; half from semi-structured Wikipedia sources

**Wikidata** serves as the largest public knowledge graph (100M+ concepts, 560K+ editors). In digital humanities, it's used for annotation/enrichment, metadata curation, knowledge modeling, and Named Entity Recognition.

### 8.2 Timeline and Visualization Tools

- **Histropedia** — 340,000 timelines, 1.5M Wikipedia articles. Free, open data. JavaScript library (HistropediaJS) available for embedding. Queries live Wikidata.
- **Chronas** — Interactive historical atlas with 50M+ data points. Shows world maps with historical boundaries, rulers, religion, culture. User-curated.
- **Aeon Timeline** — Desktop app for creating historical timelines with relationship tracking between events and entities
- **Neatline** (Scholars' Lab, UVA) — Maps + timelines for spatial/temporal interpretation. Designed for scholars and curators.
- **Time.Graphics** — Visualize events, compare processes, discover cause-and-effect relationships on interactive timelines

### 8.3 Implications for Petrarca

The existence of EventKG and Wikidata means Petrarca doesn't need to build a historical event database from scratch — it can query or import from these sources to provide temporal context, event relationships, and entity metadata. The HistoryBankQA temporal reasoning tasks (sequencing, duration, relations) provide a tested framework for generating assessment questions computationally.

## 9. Museum and Informal Learning Assessment

Museums face the same challenge as Petrarca: measuring learning in a voluntary, self-directed, curiosity-driven context where there are no grades or formal tests.

### 9.1 Assessment Methods

- **Exit interviews** with continual-ask sampling (intercept visitors as they leave)
- **Front-end evaluation** — Understand what visitors already know, their questions and misconceptions, what resonates personally
- **Timing and tracking** — Observe how long people spend with exhibits
- **Five categories of learning:** Cognitive, Affective, Social, Skill development, Personal
- **Observational approaches** alone can provide evidence of learning conditions; interviews and formal assessments are not always required

### 9.2 Key Insight: Identity-Centered Learning

John Falk's identity-centered approach to museum learning (Pacific Science Center) suggests that learning outcomes depend heavily on the visitor's *motivation type*: explorers, facilitators, experience seekers, professionals/hobbyists, and spiritual pilgrims. Each type engages differently with the same content.

**Implication for Petrarca:** As a single-user system, Petrarca can model the user's specific motivation patterns and knowledge-seeking behaviors rather than designing for generic "visitor types."

### 9.3 Self-Directed Learning Assessment

Research on adult self-directed learning (SDL) shows:
- ~90% of adults are actively involved in intentional learning
- Average adult spends 400+ hours annually in learning episodes
- ~70% of learning is informal (outside institutions)
- **Guglielmino's Self-Directed Learning Readiness Scale (SDLRS)** assesses 8 factors: openness to learning, self-concept as learner, initiative/independence, responsibility acceptance, love of learning, creativity, future orientation, study/problem-solving skills

## 10. Synthesis: Implications for Petrarca

### 10.1 What Genuine Historical Understanding Looks Like

Synthesizing all frameworks, genuine historical understanding involves multiple layers:

| Layer | What it means | How Petrarca could assess it | Current system support |
|-------|---------------|------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Factual recall** | Know key dates, people, events | Deterministic questions from key_facts | Strong (key_facts pipeline) |
| **Chronological thinking** | Place events in correct temporal order; understand duration and periodization | Timeline tasks, sequencing questions | Partial (temporal hooks) |
| **Causal reasoning** | Explain why events happened; identify multiple causes; trace consequences | "Why" questions; causal chain reconstruction | Weak (needs development) |
| **Contextual understanding** | Situate events within broader historical context | Contextualization prompts; "What else was happening?" questions | Moderate (curriculum nodes provide context) |
| **Source awareness** | Understand that accounts are constructed, not given | Questions about who says what and why; competing interpretations | Not directly assessed |
| **Historical significance** | Judge what matters and articulate why | Significance ranking tasks; "Why is X important?" questions | Not directly assessed |
| **Perspective taking** | Understand historical actors' viewpoints without presentism | Perspective-based questions; "What would X's view be?" | Not directly assessed |
| **Connection making** | Link across periods, themes, geographies | Cross-node questions; "How does X relate to Y?" | Moderate (entity links, curriculum structure) |
| **Complexity/Nuance** | Qualify arguments; see multiple sides; avoid oversimplification | Evaluate responses for AP-style complexity indicators | Not directly assessed |

### 10.2 Concrete Framework Recommendations

**A. Adapt the AP Complexity Point for review assessment.** When evaluating voice dumps or knowledge elicitation responses, check for complexity indicators:
- Does the user explain multiple causes or multiple effects?
- Do they acknowledge both continuity AND change?
- Do they make connections across periods?
- Do they qualify their claims?

**B. Use the Seixas Big Six as dimensions for knowledge state.** Each curriculum domain could have implicit sub-scores along:
- Factual knowledge (key_facts)
- Chronological framework (can they sequence events?)
- Causal understanding (can they explain why things happened?)
- Contextual richness (can they connect to broader patterns?)
- Significance awareness (do they know what matters most and why?)
- Perspective awareness (do they understand multiple viewpoints?)

**C. Apply Shemilt's framework levels to track macro-progression:**
- Level 1: Timeline knowledge (knows when things happened)
- Level 2: Narrative knowledge (can tell a coherent story)
- Level 3: Multidimensional knowledge (can analyze through multiple lenses)
- Level 4: Analytical knowledge (can compare frameworks and evaluate interpretations)

This maps onto Petrarca's knowledge levels:
- `unknown` → no framework
- `mentioned` → beginning Level 1 (some facts placed in time)
- `engaged` → Levels 1-2 (can sequence events, has narrative fragments)
- `anchored` → Levels 2-3+ (has narrative framework, makes connections)

**D. Generate "elaborative interrogation" questions.** Instead of "What year did X happen?", generate "WHY did X happen?" and "What were the consequences of X?" questions. Research shows these "why" questions dramatically improve comprehension and retention because they force the learner to connect new information to existing knowledge.

**E. Use HistoryBankQA task types for computational question generation:**
- SequenceQA: "Which came first, X or Y?"
- DurationQA: "How long did X last?"
- RelationQA: "Was X before or after Y?"
- CountQA: "How many major events occurred between X and Y?"

These are deterministically generable from structured data (key_facts with dates) and assess temporal reasoning without requiring LLM generation.

**F. Track epistemic development via Maggioni's three stances.** In voice dumps, look for indicators:
- Copier: "X happened because..." (single cause, presented as fact)
- Subjectivist: "Some people say X, others say Y, who really knows"
- Criterialist: "The evidence suggests X because..., though Y complicates this"

### 10.3 Assessment Approaches by Engagement Type

| Engagement type | Assessment approach | Framework basis |
|----------------|---------------------|-----------------|
| **Reading an article** | Claim tracking, novelty detection, delta reporting | Petrarca existing |
| **Review cards** | Key facts recall + elaborative interrogation | Bloom + elaborative interrogation research |
| **Voice dump** | Knowledge elicitation, epistemic stance detection, complexity scoring | Wineburg think-aloud, AP complexity, Maggioni |
| **Timeline interaction** | Sequencing, duration reasoning, periodization | NCHS Standard 1, HistoryBankQA |
| **Concept map viewing** | Connection identification, gap detection | Seixas (cause/consequence, continuity/change) |
